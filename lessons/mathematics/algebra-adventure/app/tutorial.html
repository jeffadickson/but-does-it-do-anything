Why use a letter when a box already works?

A box is a useful place for a missing number. Algebra gives that number a name, then gives us a consistent way to reason about it when problems become more involved.

Start with something familiar

For 2 + □ = 3, you may simply see that the box holds 1. A bar picture can show a whole of 3 split into 2 and the missing part. That is a perfectly good solution.

What changes?

Now two equal bags and three loose counters make eleven. A picture still works: remove the three loose counters and share the other eight equally. But we need to keep track of an unknown repeated twice, an extra amount, and a total.

Picture reasoningThe same reasoning in algebra
Two equal bags and three extras make eleven.2x + 3 = 11; x is the count in each bag.
Remove the three extras.2x + 3 − 3 = 11 − 3, so 2x = 8.
Share the remaining eight into two equal parts.2x ÷ 2 = 8 ÷ 2, so x = 4.
Put four counters in each bag and check the total.2 × 4 + 3 = 11.

Try it and look for evidence

  1. Open episode 2. Add to one side only: the equality breaks. Repair it. Reset and subtract from both sides: equal values stay equal.
  2. In episode 4, solve 2 + x = 3 and check by replacing x with 1.
  3. In episode 8, subtract 3, then divide by 2. Each equation step shows the new relationship. Compare it with the bag explanation above.
  4. In episode 10, try different child counts. The check reports both total and difference. A count must satisfy both clues.

The observable benefit is a reusable sequence of justified steps, with a check against the original problem. A correct click is not evidence that a child understands why it works.

Costs and when to keep it simple

Letters introduce notation to learn. Use counters or a bar when that is clearer; do not insist on several written steps for a sum the child can already explain. The aim is to connect representations and gradually use algebra when it makes reasoning easier.

Teaching routine

Try 10–15 minutes with an adult: remember, predict, explore, explain, and pause. Episodes 1–11 form the core; episode 12 is optional. Hints are welcome. A fresh explanation and a later revisit matter more than speed or counts. The first six episodes explicitly connect boxes and bars to equations.

What actually runs?

JavaScript really generates questions, evaluates answers, applies arithmetic operations and stores local progress.
