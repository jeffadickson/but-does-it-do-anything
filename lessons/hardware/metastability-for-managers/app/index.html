01 / The problem

“Ready” does not arrive by appointment.

A sensor tells a controller that its measurement is ready. The sensor and controller run on separate clocks. The controller must turn that outside signal into a decision on its own schedule. Most arrivals look uneventful. A rare arrival near the decision instant is the interesting one.

Three ideas you need

Clock: a repeating timing signal. Its rising edge is the instant our circuit samples an input.

Flip-flop: a tiny memory element that samples one bit (0 or 1), then holds it.

Sampling window: a brief interval before and after the edge when the input must stay steady. Engineers call these the setup and hold requirements.

What goes wrong?

If the input changes inside that window, the flip-flop may take longer than expected to produce a valid 0 or 1. That physical condition is metastability.

If exposed to the rest of the circuit, a late or unsettled result can lead to inconsistent decisions. A controller might act on “ready” in one place while another part has not recognized it.

A window violation is a risk, not a promise that every sample fails. Neither slow clocks nor a clean-looking lab test prove the crossing is safe.

Your goal: explain the risk, explain what a synchronizer buys, recognize when it is insufficient, and ask the engineering team for the right evidence. No transistor physics or timing equations needed.

A visible consequence / one edge, wrong number

It should count 3 → 4. Why did it show 7?

Now use an unsafe READY signal as the count-enable for a four-bit counter. The clock itself is clean. On one edge the counter should either hold at 3, or increment once to 4. If the enable arrives near that edge—or is an unsettled first-stage output—different counter bits can make inconsistent hold/update decisions.

Binary 3 is 0011; binary 4 is 0100. Suppose bit 2 updates but bits 1 and 0 hold. The result is 0111 = 7: an apparent jump of four from just one clock edge.

03 / The remedy

The second flip-flop buys settling time.

At the first receiving edge

FF1 samples the outside signal and may become metastable. FF2 samples FF1's previous value. The new value does not race through both stages on one edge.

Between receiving edges

FF1 gets time to settle. Ordinary logic uses FF2, not FF1. This is the central design choice.

At the next receiving edge

If FF1 settled with enough timing margin, FF2 captures a valid 0 or 1. That may be the old value; a persistent READY level can be recognized on a later cycle.

What the project gains—and pays

Gain: a much lower likelihood that unresolved metastability reaches ordinary logic.

Cost: extra registers and latency. Recognition is not instantaneous or an exact fixed delay from an asynchronous arrival.

Limit: two stages do not guarantee success. Stage count must meet the actual reliability target using the actual clocks, device and implementation.

Implementation matters: long routing or logic between stages consumes settling time. Engineers use recognized synchronizer structures, placement guidance and timing analysis.

A two-flip-flop synchronizer has two receiving-clock stages. A dual-clock system has two clock domains. These are different ideas: the two synchronizer stages are not clocked one from each domain.

Why “it ran all weekend” is not a reliability argument

A test samples a limited set of timing alignments and conditions. A rare failure can escape it. Engineering estimates often use mean time between failures (MTBF), a statistical quantity rather than a promise about any individual unit. Ask whether the analysis includes the real clock and transition rates, available settling time, operating conditions and all relevant crossings in the system.

Engineering sidebar · RTG4 / Libero implementation

Manager takeaway: two boxes in RTL are not enough. Preserve the stages, place them close, then inspect routed timing. RTG4 groups logic into clusters of 12 logic elements; “same island” is not a substitute for checking the actual FF1-to-FF2 path.

Show synthesis attributes and placement calls

1. Synplify synthesis: an illustrative Verilog register declaration uses these documented preservation controls. These are not placement locks.

// Inside a module; clk_b is the receiving clock.
reg ff1 /* synthesis syn_preserve=1 syn_replicate=0 syn_allow_retiming=0 */;
reg ff2 /* synthesis syn_preserve=1 syn_replicate=0 syn_allow_retiming=0 */;
always @(posedge clk_b) begin
    ff1 <= ready_async;
    ff2 <= ff1;
end
// Ordinary logic uses ff2 only.

syn_preserve=1 retains register logic; syn_replicate=0 prevents duplication; syn_allow_retiming=0 prevents retiming. Confirm support and acceptance in the bundled Synplify version and inspect the resulting netlist. Reset/startup behavior is omitted from this fragment and must be designed separately.

2. Physical placement: RTG4's Libero v2025.2 PDC reference gives the following command forms. Substitute real post-synthesis macro names and legal coordinates from Chip Planner; the angle-bracket fields below are placeholders, not executable values.

# Lock the two cells at selected nearby legal sites:
set_location {u_ready/ff1} -fixed yes <X1> <Y1>
set_location {u_ready/ff2} -fixed yes <X2> <Y2>

# Alternative: constrain both cells to a small legal region.
# This restricts the area; it does not lock exact sites.
define_region -name ready_sync -type inclusive <Xmin> <Ymin> <Xmax> <Ymax>
assign_region ready_sync {u_ready/ff1} {u_ready/ff2}

Use either the exact-site approach or a suitably small region. Chip Planner can export the PDC for your actual part and constraint flow. Illustrative names need not survive synthesis unchanged. Add the floorplanning constraint file to Place and Route, inspect accepted constraints, and verify the resulting route. This example was documentation-checked, not run in Libero.

3. Verify the available settling time: include routing, clock-to-output, setup and clock timing effects. Keep the FF1-to-FF2 path timed; do not hide it with a blanket false-path exception. Close placement helps preserve time, but does not determine the required stage count by itself.

Microchip AN6287 recommends more than two stages for RTG4 above 125 MHz. That is not a guarantee that two stages suffice below 125 MHz: use the real transition rate, device conditions, SET-filter configuration and system reliability target.

04 / Clock-domain crossing, without the rabbit hole

Choose the crossing for the message.

A clock domain is a group of circuitry following a clock. When information moves between domains, the receiving side needs a valid timing and transfer arrangement. Even equal nominal clock frequencies can drift in phase if their sources are independent. Clocks with a known, properly constrained relationship can instead be analyzed as synchronous paths.

What crosses?Typical approachWhat to ask
A persistent one-bit status
“Ready” stays asserted		A receiving-clock synchronizerDoes the level last long enough, and can the receiver tolerate latency?
An event / brief pulseA suitable pulse/toggle protocol or acknowledged handshakeCan events be missed or arrive faster than the protocol accepts? A short pulse can occur entirely between samples.
A whole command or data wordA mailbox with handshakeIs the word held stable while a synchronized request crosses, and is acknowledgement returned before reuse?
A stream of data wordsAn asynchronous (dual-clock) FIFOAre reads/writes controlled by valid empty/full information, with enough capacity or backpressure?

Mailbox: think of one held message with a “received” reply. FIFO: think of a queue that preserves word order while the producer and consumer run on separate clocks. Both need properly designed CDC control; neither is just an arbitrary register array.

Do not synchronize each bit of a changing binary word independently and assume the result is coherent. Bits can be observed on different cycles, producing a word the sender never intended.

Reset release also needs domain-appropriate treatment. That, FIFO internals and detailed timing constraints belong in a follow-on engineering lesson.

05 / The design-review conversation

Ask for evidence, not reassurance.

  1. “Show me the crossings.” A reviewed inventory of asynchronous inputs and clock/reset domains.
  2. “What exactly is being transferred?” Level, event, coherent word or stream—and the matching protocol.
  3. “Where is uncertainty contained?” Recognized synchronizer structures; no ordinary logic fed from the first stage.
  4. “What is the reliability basis?” Device-specific timing/MTBF analysis and implementation checks against the system target.
  5. “What did verification cover?” CDC analysis, reviewed exceptions, protocol checks and relevant tests. A clean functional simulation alone is insufficient.

Return to our sensor: a held READY bit can use a suitable synchronizer. Transferring the measurement itself needs a coherent data-transfer scheme. The small circuit buys time; the protocol ensures the right information arrives.

Quick reference & sources

Five terms worth keeping

Metastability: a physical unsettled state that can delay a valid output.
Setup/hold: input-stability requirements around sampling.
Synchronizer: a structure that reduces propagation of metastability across a sampling boundary.
CDC: clock-domain crossing.
MTBF: mean time between failures under specified assumptions.
