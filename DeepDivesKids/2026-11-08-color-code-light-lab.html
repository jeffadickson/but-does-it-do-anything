The mystery

Why does red + green make yellow on a screen?

Quinn is designing a glowing scoreboard. Reagan wants a bright gold victory flash. They switch on the red and green parts of a pixel—and the screen looks yellow. Wait … red and green paint usually don't do that!

Your mission: Build yellow using light, solve three color challenges, and learn why scientists sometimes paint forests red in satellite pictures. No actual paint is needed for the computer part.

Two different recipes are at work: screens add light, while paints and markers absorb some light and reflect the rest. That's why mixing them gives different results.

Decode the science

Three dials, many colors

🔴 Red channel

A number from 0 (off) to 255 (maximum in our 8-bit color model).

🟢 Green channel

Another 0–255 number. Red plus green at maximum, with no blue, produces bright yellow on a typical display.

🔵 Blue channel

Also 0–255. All three at 0 give black; all three at 255 give white in standard RGB coding.

One screen color can be written RGB(255, 255, 0). That means red is fully on, green is fully on, and blue is off. A shorter computer spelling is #FFFF00: two hexadecimal digits for each channel.

Your eyes aren't literally tiny RGB meters. The National Eye Institute explains that humans generally have three kinds of color-sensitive cone cells, most sensitive to different ranges of light wavelengths. Your brain helps turn those signals into color experiences. See the eye-science evidence ↗

Important detail: this is a useful digital color model, not a claim that every screen has identical red-green-blue stripes or that real lights mix perfectly. Display hardware and color perception are more complicated.

Brain teaser: How many combinations can three 8-bit channels make?

Reveal the count

There are 256 choices for each of three channels, so 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 possible number combinations. Displays may not show every one as a different-looking color to every person.

Lab 03 · Real science, pretend data

Why might a forest look red from space?

NASA explains that satellites can measure colors your eyes can't see, such as near-infrared light. Scientists can assign that invisible measurement to the red part of a picture, making healthy plant-covered areas appear red. That is called a false-color image—and it can reveal information hidden in an ordinary-looking picture.

Hands-on · 5 minutes

Light recipe versus marker recipe

You'll need white paper, a red marker, a green marker, and this page. Use washable markers and a surface you can color on.

  1. On the computer, press Sun yellow in the mixer: R=255, G=255, B=0. Write down the resulting name, “yellow.”
  2. On paper, draw a solid red patch. Add green marker over half of it. What happens to the covered part?
  3. Ask: “Did I add light with the markers, or did I add material that absorbs some light?”
  4. Try Ice cyan and Magic magenta on the computer. Can marker layers copy the exact look?
  5. Explain the result in one sentence to someone at home.
Prediction: Overlapping red and green marker ink usually gets darker or muddier than glowing yellow. The exact result depends on which markers you used. Paints and inks do not obey the simple RGB-screen rule.

Coding corner · For curious builders

Three numbers turn into one color

Below is the same core idea as the working mixer on this page. It uses the browser's built-in Canvas 2D API, not an external library. Save this example as colors.html and open it in a browser, or just play with the live mixer above.

<canvas id="color" width="240" height="100"
  aria-label="A yellow rectangle">
  A yellow rectangle.
</canvas>
<script>
  const red = 255;
  const green = 255;
  const blue = 0;
  const canvas = document.getElementById("color");
  const ctx = canvas.getContext("2d");
  if (ctx) {
    ctx.fillStyle = "rgb(" + red + "," + green + "," + blue + ")";
    ctx.fillRect(0, 0, canvas.width, canvas.height);
  }
</script>

Try changing blue = 0 to blue = 255. You should turn the rectangle white. The HTML Living Standard defines Canvas; MDN explains fillStyle.

Make it your own

Replace the rectangle with a rink scoreboard, pixel creature, or comic-book power effect. Add input controls and redraw when their values change, just like this page.

Dad's engineering connection

RISCue handles events—like a button or sensor saying “something changed.” This page also reacts to events when you move a slider. VeloSim makes repeatable computer simulations. Evidence versus imagination

What's real? What's our invention?

✅ Verified real uses

Color vision: NEI describes three cone types in human eyes, each sensitive to different wavelength ranges.

Earth observation: NASA explains how satellite bands are assigned to RGB channels to make real natural- and false-color imagery. USGS documents the Landsat band numbers.

Web graphics: the HTML Canvas standard specifies drawing operations; the color-mixer code uses them to draw rectangles.

💡 Invented for this lesson

The scoreboard quest, “golden goal” story, pixel microscope illustration, fictional forest/town grid, and exact satellite pixel values are educational models.

A hockey scoreboard art editor or RISCue-powered color controller could be a future project. We did not verify such a product or integration.

What can't our simple model do?

  • Light is complicated. Actual red, green and blue LEDs have spectra; the same RGB numbers may look a little different on different displays.
  • Eyes differ. Some people perceive colors differently, and some colors are difficult to distinguish. That's why the controls also use names and numerical readouts.
  • Pixels differ. The giant stripe picture is a teaching illustration. Real screens can use different pixel patterns.
  • Science needs calibration. NASA satellite pictures use measured bands, selected display ranges and image processing; our little invented grid has none of that.
  • Software needs testing. Sliders and Canvas make learning easy, but they don't automatically make flight-qualified or real-time control software.

Quick check

Question A: Which recipe makes white in standard RGB?

R=255, G=255, B=255. All three channels are at maximum.

Question B: Can a satellite display invisible light as red?

Yes. The measured invisible band can be assigned to the red display channel, which is how a false-color image can be built. The forest itself hasn't changed color.

Question C: Is our pretend map actual Landsat data?

No. It uses made-up numbers to show the idea; scientific images use measurements from real instruments.
