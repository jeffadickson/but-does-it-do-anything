Case file 01 · The mountain mystery

Why does Yosemite Valley look like a giant letter U?

Quinn and Reagan, picture standing among Yosemite's immense granite walls. If a small river cuts down into a mountain, it often leaves a narrow, V-shaped valley. But Yosemite Valley has especially steep sides and a broad floor. Something much bigger visited long ago: glaciers.

🌊 River: the V maker

Flowing water can cut a narrow channel deeper over time. Look across the valley: the sides slope toward a pointed low spot, like V.

🧊 Glacier: the U maker

Thick, moving ice can grind and pull rock from the floor and the sides, widening and deepening the valley. The profile can become more like a U.

Evidence: The National Park Service identifies both Yosemite Valley and Lyell Canyon as U-shaped valleys shaped by glaciers. Streams had already carved valleys before ice widened and deepened them. Read the park's geology explanation.

Case file 02 · The mechanism

How can frozen water move?

01

Snow piles up

Year after year, deep snow can squash into ice. If the ice becomes thick enough, its own weight makes it deform and flow.

02

Gravity pulls

The ice slowly creeps downhill. Some glaciers also slide at their bases. This is not a giant ice cube skating in one piece!

03

Rock does the scraping

Rocks trapped in the bottom ice scratch the bedrock (abrasion). Ice can also lift loose chunks of rock (plucking).

Primary evidence: USGS: what makes glaciers move; NPS: abrasion and plucking.

Case file 03 · Verified discoveries

Three clues scientists actually use

🏞️ Yosemite's giant U

The National Park Service explains that glaciers widened and deepened older stream valleys. At the Tioga glaciation's maximum, about 18,000 years ago, ice covered many high parts of Yosemite.

NPS Yosemite geology

🪨 Scratches in rock

Real scientists study straight scratches called striations. Their direction helps show which way ice moved. The scratches are evidence that the rock was scraped, not a treasure-map arrow!

NPS glacial striations

🛰️ Space measures ice

NASA's ICESat-2, launched in 2018, uses precisely timed green laser pulses to measure Earth's changing surface heights, including ice. Its laser fires 10,000 pulses each second.

NASA mission · NASA instrument details

🔬 A real JPL investigation

In a study reported by JPL in December 2018, researchers compared almost two million satellite images and found that many high-mountain glaciers in Asia were flowing more slowly as they lost ice. This shows how photographs and careful measurements help test scientific ideas. Read the original JPL report.

Hands-on · grown-up helper recommended

🧪 The tiny glacier scratch test

Let's test one part of the glacier story: can gritty ice scrape a softer surface? This is a tabletop analogy, not an experiment that duplicates kilometers of glacial ice.

Gather

Two ice cubes, a pinch of clean sand, a small washable slab of modeling clay, a tray, a towel and paper for your observations. Ask an adult to help freeze a little sand into one cube ahead of time.

Safety: Work on a tray, keep sand out of food and eyes, and wash hands afterward. Do not rub grit on furniture or natural park rocks.

Try it

  1. Flatten the clay into two equally smooth patches.
  2. Slide the plain ice cube across patch A five times with gentle pressure.
  3. Slide the sandy ice cube across patch B five times with about the same pressure.
  4. Compare marks. Photograph or draw what you see, even if the results are surprising.

What would count as evidence? Record actual scratches and whether loose sand touched the clay. If both patches look the same, write that down! The NPS describes real glacial abrasion, but this little test only demonstrates what grit might do on clay; it does not measure how glaciers cut granite.

Reality check · Dream big, label it clearly

Real engineering vs. invented ideas

✅ Real technology

ICESat-2 measures heights by timing tiny flashes of reflected light. Scientists also compare satellite images to estimate glacier motion. These are documented NASA/JPL uses, not pretend gadgets.

🏒 From Tahoe ice to Dad's engineering desk

At the hockey rink, Quinn skates on hard, prepared ice while Reagan guards the goal. A glacier is different: it is immensely thicker, moves because of gravity, and can carry rocks. Both stories ask you to notice motion and forces.

🧩 How this page is built

Just HTML + CSS + vanilla JavaScript + inline SVG. No external JavaScript libraries, fonts, images, network calls, API keys or accounts. Save this single HTML file and open it in a modern browser; the interactive valley should work offline.

Licensing & integration: The code uses browser-native standards; it adds no third-party code with separate package license requirements. The original lesson drawings and text are not licensed by the government agencies linked above. Reuse of outside photographs or datasets would require checking their own terms. To integrate this page, simply link to the HTML or serve it as a static file; there is no npm install or build system.

Tradeoffs: Easy to run and inspect, but simplified graphics cannot solve real glaciology; it has no real elevation datasets, satellite processing or calibrated time scale. That limitation is part of being a good scientist.
