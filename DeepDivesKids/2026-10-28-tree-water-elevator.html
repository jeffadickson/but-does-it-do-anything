01 · A surprising mystery

No heart. No hose. No problem?

02 · Follow one water drop

The science behind a tree's water elevator

🌱

1. Roots collect

Roots absorb water from soil. It enters special plant tissue called xylem (say “ZY-lem”), which transports water and dissolved minerals.

💧

2. Water holds together

Water molecules attract each other: that's cohesion. Their attraction to other surfaces is adhesion. A connected column of water can transmit a pull.

🍃

3. Leaves lose water

Water evaporates inside leaves and vapor leaves through tiny pores called stomata. This transpiration helps create tension that pulls water upward through the xylem.

Important: Capillary action helps water move through narrow spaces, but capillary action alone cannot explain water reaching the tops of tall trees. Scientists use the cohesion–tension mechanism to explain long-distance water movement. See the Forest Service's research summary.

Picture hockey teammates holding hands in a long line. A tug at one end can be felt by others. Water molecules aren't teammates, but their cohesion makes this a useful first picture of a connected water column.

04 · Hands-on discovery

🧪 Make celery reveal its invisible plumbing

Get ready

You'll need two clear glasses, water, a few drops of food coloring, two similar fresh celery stalks with leaves, paper towels and a notebook. Ask an adult to handle any cutting.

Setup: about 10 minutes. Observation: several hours or longer, depending on the celery.

Make it a fair test

Put one celery stalk in plain water (your control) and another in colored water. Keep both in the same location. Predict which stalk's pathways will be easier to see.

Be safe: Food dye stains. Do not drink experiment water. Only an adult should cut the celery. Wash hands after handling coloring.
  1. Fill each glass with water. Color only one glass with several drops of food dye.
  2. Place a celery stalk into each glass, with its cut base underwater. Write down the starting time.
  3. Check again after an hour, then later. Sketch what you actually see, even if nothing appears yet.
  4. Have an adult cut a thin cross-section of the dyed stalk near its base. Look for colored spots where water-carrying tissue is located.
  5. Finish the scientist sentence: “We predicted ___; we observed ___; next time we would change ___.”
Extra challenge: With permission, loosely place a clear bag over a few leaves on a healthy outdoor plant for a short observation, and remove it afterward. Avoid hot direct sun and don't damage the plant. Do you see condensation? This demonstrates vapor leaving leaves; it does not directly measure xylem tension. USGS shows this experiment.

05 · Reality vs imagination

🚀 From backyard plants to space experiments

REAL · NASA documentation

Plants really grow in experiments aboard the ISS

NASA's Advanced Plant Habitat is an actual, automated plant-growth chamber on the International Space Station. NASA explains how its lighting and growing substrate help deliver water, nutrients and oxygen to roots. Keeping roots healthy is particularly challenging in microgravity. This is real space research—not a claim that NASA uses our simulation. NASA primary source.

REAL · Scientific research

Scientists investigate forces in xylem

The U.S. Forest Service has published research explaining the cohesion–tension theory of water transport and how evaporation at leaves generates pulling force. Read their summary.

Make a tiny plant monitor

You could build a sensor system that records light and humidity by a plant. Dad's RISCue small-computer work or VeloSim simulation ideas could inspire how to test the readings and make predictions. That would be a new suggested project—not a claimed feature of Dad's existing projects and not a NASA flight device.

06 · A real program with honest limits

💻 Teach a computer a pretend tree

The interactive lab uses ordinary JavaScript, not a downloaded library. This is the actual score function used by the game. Same inputs, same output, every time—something engineers call deterministic.

function waterPullScore(sun, breeze, humidity) {
  const warmth = 0.25 + 0.75 * sun / 100;
  const airflow = 0.65 + 0.35 * breeze / 100;
  const dryAir = 1 - 0.75 * humidity / 100;
  return Math.round(10 + 80 * warmth * airflow * dryAir);
}

// These are MADE-UP scores, not scientific measurements.
waterPullScore(60, 40, 30); // 44

Code challenge: Predict whether waterPullScore(60, 40, 100) will be higher or lower than 44. Then change only the humidity slider to test your prediction.

✅ Why this approach is useful

It is small, quick, offline, inspectable, and requires no external JavaScript packages, accounts, trackers, servers or network access. A saved copy works in modern browsers.

⚠️ Where it falls short

These weights are invented. A real plant model would need measured parameters, soil moisture, stomatal regulation, species data and physical units. No real plant is being measured here.

07 · Test your brain

Three mini-mysteries

1. Is a tree secretly using a mechanical pump like a car?

No. The cohesion–tension mechanism, driven largely by evaporation from leaves, helps move water through xylem. A tree has no mechanical pump like a car engine.

2. Why might a tree lose less water into very humid air?

It can be harder for water to evaporate into air that is already moist. Real stomata also open and close.

3. Is the number 44 a measured quantity?

No! It is an invented score. Real measurements need instruments, units and careful comparisons.

Words worth knowing

Your scientist's pocket dictionary

Xylem
Plant tissue that transports water and dissolved minerals.
Stomata
Tiny pores, usually in leaves, that regulate exchange with air.
Transpiration
Water escaping plants as water vapor, often through leaves.
Cohesion
Attraction between molecules of the same substance.
Control
A comparison used to make an experiment fair.
Model
A simplified picture or calculation that explains some things but not everything.
