01 / Mission briefing

A space station is falling. So why doesn't it land?

Quinn and Reagan, picture throwing a puck sideways. It moves forward, but gravity pulls it toward the ground. Now imagine an impossibly powerful throw, high above the air, where Earth curves away beneath the puck.

It might keep missing the ground, again and again, all the way around the planet. That's an orbit! An actual satellite doesn't get its speed from a giant hockey stick. A rocket carries it up and gives it the right sideways velocity.

The big surprise: Orbiting doesn't mean gravity disappears. At the International Space Station, Earth is still pulling strongly. The spacecraft and astronauts are falling together, which makes them float relative to one another. NASA calls this microgravity. NASA explains why.

02 / How it works

Three ingredients in the space sandwich

➡️1. Sideways speed

An object moving through space keeps traveling in a straight line unless a force changes its motion.

🌎2. Earth's gravity

Earth pulls the satellite toward Earth's center, bending its path at every moment.

🛰️3. The perfect miss

At the right speed, the curve of the fall matches the curve of Earth. The satellite circles without touching the ground.

At a starting height of 400 kilometers, our ideal circular-orbit speed is about 7.67 kilometers per second. That is more than 27,000 kilometers per hour! The satellite completes a lap in roughly 92 minutes. The International Space Station travels at similar speeds and heights, according to the European Space Agency.

Try a prediction: Too slow, and the spacecraft may hit Earth. A little faster than circular speed, and it can trace a stretched oval (an ellipse). Faster still, it can escape Earth altogether.

04 / Try it yourself

The four-flight challenge + a paper experiment

Mission cards — can you beat all four?
  1. Choose Too slow. Predict whether the satellite will go once around Earth. Launch and watch.
  2. Choose Circle. Can you make one complete lap without touching Earth?
  3. Choose Oval. Look for the satellite speeding up when closer to Earth and slowing down farther away.
  4. Choose Escape. Does the satellite make a closed loop, or head outward?

Super-engineer challenge: Find the slowest speed that can avoid Earth in this ideal simulation. Change the slider a little at a time and use the one-minute step button to test your prediction.

✏️ Off-screen experiment: draw a satellite's next move

You need: a sheet of paper, pencil, ruler, and a cup to trace a circle. No throwing anything!

Trace a big circle for Earth. Put a dot just outside its edge on the right. Draw a straight arrow up from the dot: that is the direction the satellite wants to travel. Then draw another arrow left, toward Earth's center: that is gravity's pull. Connect several small forward moves, bending each one a bit toward Earth. The resulting curve is a rough sketch, not an exact orbital calculation.

Investigator question: Why does the satellite change direction if nobody is steering? Because gravity is changing its velocity all the time.

Quick brain-check

1. Is there gravity up where the astronauts live?

Yes! Gravity keeps the space station orbiting. The astronauts float because they and the station fall together.

2. If a satellite moves slightly faster than circular speed, must it escape?

No. It can travel in an oval orbit. It needs the higher local escape speed (about 10.85 km/s at our starting height) to escape in this simplified model.

3. Is the path in this game accurate enough to fly a real spacecraft?

No. Real mission navigation includes more forces, precise measurements, spacecraft engines, and careful safety checks.

06 / Code lab + engineering honesty

How does the browser know where to fly?

We use ordinary JavaScript and the browser's built-in Canvas 2D API. There is no external library or internet dependency. All positions are in kilometers and times in seconds. We start with NASA's approximate Earth numbers: mean radius 6,371 km and gravitational parameter 398,600 km³/s².

Each little fixed step asks: Which way is Earth pulling? Then it updates the satellite's position and speed. The live simulator uses a velocity-Verlet numerical step, which is more accurate for orbits than a naïve one-line position update.

// Positions in km, speeds in km/s, time in seconds.
const GM = 398600;
function acceleration(x, y) {
  const r = Math.hypot(x, y);
  const factor = -GM / (r * r * r);
  return {x: factor * x, y: factor * y};
}
function advance(s, dt) {
  const a = acceleration(s.x, s.y);
  const x = s.x + s.vx * dt + 0.5 * a.x * dt * dt;
  const y = s.y + s.vy * dt + 0.5 * a.y * dt * dt;
  const b = acceleration(x, y);
  return {x, y,
    vx: s.vx + 0.5 * (a.x + b.x) * dt,
    vy: s.vy + 0.5 * (a.y + b.y) * dt};
}
// Same start + speed + step count = same calculated path.

Try modifying the dt time step in your own copy of the page. A smaller step usually gives a more accurate numerical orbit, but requires more calculations. That's an example of a tradeoff engineers make.
