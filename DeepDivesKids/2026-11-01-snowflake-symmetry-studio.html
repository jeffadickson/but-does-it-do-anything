1. Six directions hide inside a snowflake 🔍

Think about the prettiest snowflake you've seen. It might have six branches, like a tiny frozen star. If a full turn is 360 degrees, six equal turns are 360 ÷ 6 = 60 degrees each. A shape that matches itself after a 60° turn has six-fold rotational symmetry.

Careful, scientist: Not all snow is a six-pointed star. Real snow includes plates, needles, columns and clumps. Caltech crystal researcher Kenneth Libbrecht explains that most real crystals are not perfectly symmetrical.

2. How snow crystals really grow

Step 1: A tiny seed

In a cloud, water vapor can change directly into solid ice on a tiny particle. Water molecules arrange themselves in an orderly, hexagonal crystal structure. Hexagonal means six-sided.

Step 2: More molecules arrive

The tiny ice crystal grows. Some conditions favor flat plates; others favor columns, needles, or branched forms.

Step 3: Corners can branch

Water vapor collects on a growing crystal. Small protrusions can grow faster and form branches. When six arms experience similar conditions, they often grow into similar shapes.

Step 4: The weather changes

As the crystal travels through different temperatures and humidity, its shape changes. The arms do not communicate: they simply experience similar weather. Small differences make them uneven.

Important distinction: Our drawing repeats the exact same line using math. Real crystals grow molecule by molecule, with many imperfections.

4. One tiny loop, six beautiful directions 💻

Imagine a robot artist: draw this line, rotate, repeat. In computer science, repeated instructions are called a loop. Here is a working JavaScript pattern using the browser's Canvas 2D API:

function drawSixCopies(ctx, points, center) {
  for (let arm = 0; arm < 6; arm++) {
    ctx.save();
    ctx.translate(center, center);
    ctx.rotate(arm * Math.PI / 3); // 60 degrees
    ctx.beginPath();
    ctx.moveTo(points[0].x, points[0].y);
    for (let i = 1; i < points.length; i++) {
      ctx.lineTo(points[i].x, points[i].y);
    }
    ctx.stroke();
    ctx.restore();
  }
}

Try changing the rule: Four equally spaced directions use a quarter-circle turn: 2 * Math.PI / 4. Six directions use 2 * Math.PI / 6. The studio itself uses this rotation trick, plus Undo and optional mirror copies. The angles are in radians, another way to measure turns.

Why save() and restore()? Each copy should begin from the same center. These commands prevent one turn from accidentally piling onto the next turn.

6. Try it without a computer ✂️

Gather one piece of paper, a ruler, pencil and crayons. A protractor helps, but you can estimate the spacing.

  1. Draw a dot in the center of the page.
  2. Draw six straight lines pointing outward, with approximately 60° between neighboring lines.
  3. Add three little branches to one arm. Copy the same branches onto the other five arms.
  4. Make a second drawing, but change one branch on one arm.
  5. Compare your exact-copy artwork to your not-quite-symmetric drawing. Which took more careful work?

Prediction: Which is faster: copying the twigs by hand or letting the computer repeat them? This activity models the symmetry, not how water molecules actually freeze.

10. What this model gets right—and wrong

✅ What is real

Six evenly spaced directions are 60° apart. A computer can exactly rotate and repeat a drawing. Hexagonal molecular ordering helps explain classic six-fold ice crystals. Snow crystal shapes change with conditions.

⚠️ What is simplified

Our studio does not model humidity, air currents, temperature, microscopic molecules, growth speed or three-dimensional forms. Perfect mirroring is an artist's shortcut, not the usual result of real crystal growth. Six-pointed stars are not the only kinds of snow.
