Case 01 • The mystery

Can two pictures tell a fib?

👀 In the real world

A wheel with six identical spokes spins clockwise. Every instant it moves forward. Just like a hockey player going around the rink, it never changes direction.

📷 Inside the camera

Videos are made of separate frames (pictures taken at moments in time). What happened between pictures might be missing! Sometimes the pictures seem to show the wrong direction. Engineers call this aliasing. [1]

Case 02 • The secret

Six spokes, six disguises

A wheel with six identical spokes looks exactly the same after a turn of 60 degrees. The next spoke has replaced the previous spoke, like six friends wearing matching costumes.

360° ÷ 6 = 60°

There are 360 degrees in a full circle. Divide by six identical spokes: every 60 degrees the pattern repeats.

+45° ≡ −15°

In a snapshot, a 45-degree step forward looks exactly like a 15-degree step backward. Both produce the same six-spoke pattern.

Detective warning: The wheel itself NEVER goes backward. The camera pictures are incomplete clues. If one spoke had a special sticker, the six spokes would no longer look identical—and the mystery might be easier to solve.

Case 03 • Three missions

Can you outsmart the camera?

  1. Backward wheel: Choose 50 RPM and 8 fps, then Start. One frame arrives after the wheel advances 37.5°, which looks like 22.5° backward.
  2. Save the replay: Keep 50 RPM, but change to 24 fps. Do the wheel and the camera now seem to agree? More frames can reveal more of the motion.
  3. Freeze time: Choose 80 RPM and 8 fps. Every frame lands 60° later—one exact spoke gap. The wheel looks stopped, even though it's spinning!

Facts vs inventions

Do real engineers work with this?

📚 Real fact: the wagon-wheel illusion

An MIT computational photography text explains how filming repeating motion can produce the wagon-wheel effect: a moving wheel looks slow, stopped, or backward. This is the real science behind our lab. [1]

🔬 Real technology: industrial cameras

Sony documents image sensors intended for fast inspection of precision objects. In a June 2024 product announcement, Sony listed a global-shutter sensor with a mode of up to 134 fps. Those are manufacturer specifications, not a promise that any camera can capture every movement perfectly. [2]

🏒 Real hockey connection

In June 2025, the NHL and Sony announced a technology partnership to expand uses of Hawk-Eye measurement and tracking and NHL EDGE puck and player tracking. The partnership is documented; we are not claiming the NHL uses this wheel demo or any particular frame rate. [3]

Hands-on • No computer

✏️ Make a pocket flipbook

Find five or more sticky notes, a ruler and pencil. Ask an adult to help with scissors if needed. No fans, motors, or bright flashing lights required.

  1. Draw an identical circle at the same spot on every card. Draw six evenly spaced identical spokes.
  2. On each new card rotate the entire spoke pattern about 45° clockwise relative to the previous card. A protractor helps, but approximate drawings are fine.
  3. Stack and flip the cards in order. Because a six-spoke pattern repeats every 60°, it can look like it moves 15° backward per picture.
  4. Try putting a small special mark on the same actual spoke in every picture. Does the extra clue help your brain see what's really happening?

Experiment honesty: A real flipbook's effect depends on accurate drawing, timing, and your eyes. If you don't see it, use the on-page lab: it makes mathematically exact snapshots. Never use spinning powered fan blades for this experiment.

Peek under the hood

💻 A real piece of working code

This page uses built-in JavaScript and SVG, with no external coding library. It also uses the browser's requestAnimationFrame() to update the continuous wheel. Mozilla documents that animation timing depends on the screen refresh rate and can pause in background tabs. [4]

// Six identical spokes look the same every 60 degrees.
function apparentRpm(realRpm, framesPerSecond, spokes = 6) {
  const gap = 360 / spokes;
  const realStep = realRpm * 6 / framesPerSecond;
  const shortStep =
    ((realStep + gap / 2) % gap + gap) % gap - gap / 2;
  return shortStep * framesPerSecond / 6;
}
console.log(apparentRpm(50, 8));  // -30 (looks backward)
console.log(apparentRpm(50, 24)); //  50 (looks forward)
console.log(apparentRpm(80, 8));  //   0 (looks frozen)

Why multiply RPM by six? One turn is 360 degrees, and one minute is 60 seconds. So a wheel turning at 1 RPM moves 360 ÷ 60 = 6 degrees per second.

Super-important: This math finds the shortest-looking change, not necessarily the true direction or speed. At exactly half the gap, forward and backward guesses are equally possible.

The honest engineer check

What our camera cannot do

✅ It really models

  • Clockwise motion at a constant speed.
  • Six truly identical spokes.
  • Perfectly sharp, evenly spaced snapshots.
  • Repeatable angles and times.

⚠️ It leaves out

  • Motion blur caused by long exposures.
  • Rolling shutters reading rows at different times.
  • Different-looking spokes, shaky cameras, light and noise.
  • All the extra clues people use to understand real video.

More frames usually help reveal motion, but they don't guarantee the correct answer for every spinning pattern. Some very different actual speeds can make the same snapshots. Our lab even labels one kind of tie as ambiguous.
