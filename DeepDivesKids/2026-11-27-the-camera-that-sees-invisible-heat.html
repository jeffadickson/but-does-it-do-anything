01 · Discover

What if your eyes could see warmth?

Imagine walking out of an ice rink into a sunny parking lot. Your skates, the asphalt and a shady tree might all look ordinary. But each is sending out invisible light! Objects above absolute zero emit thermal radiation. Some of that radiation is infrared, beyond the colors our eyes can see. A thermal camera detects a particular range of this light.

1. Collect

A special lens directs infrared radiation onto a detector.

2. Calculate

Electronics turn detector signals into numbers. Calibration and knowledge about the surface help estimate its temperature.

3. Color

A computer gives numbers colors. Blue might mean cool and red warm. The colors are chosen by people, not emitted as red or blue light.

Vocabulary: infrared = light beyond visible red; pixel = one tiny picture element; calibration = checking an instrument against known references; emissivity = how effectively a surface emits thermal radiation.

A shiny metal object can reflect infrared from other things, making its apparent temperature misleading. A thermal image generally describes surfaces, not air temperature or what is hidden inside an object.

Map trick: If you stretch each picture separately, the warmest tile may turn red even at night. Two red tiles on differently scaled maps may have different temperatures. Always read the legend.

03 · Evidence

Real missions, real measurements

JPL: Los Angeles heat

On August 14, 2020, ECOSTRESS aboard the International Space Station measured a peak land surface temperature of 53.5 °C (128.3 °F) in the San Fernando Valley. Its map also detected the Ranch fire near Azusa. This is a surface temperature, not the weather forecast.

See the actual JPL heat map

JPL: the night shift

In 2018 ECOSTRESS observations of Los Angeles showed that roofs cooled faster at night than many roads. JPL explained that different surfaces store and release heat differently. The lab above borrows that idea, but none of its numbers come from these images.

Read the mission report

USGS: water detectives

In 2025 USGS released monthly estimates of water leaving land and plants for the air across the contiguous United States for 2000–2020. The model uses Landsat thermal measurements, together with other information. It helps study irrigation and drought.

Read the official dataset description

Real engineering: Landsat 8's TIRS instrument uses two thermal infrared wavelength ranges and checks its calibration using a reference heat source and a view of cold space. Its original thermal sampling is about 100 meters across, not enough to distinguish individual skaters. NASA instrument documentation.

Ideas we have not tested

Imagine a rink technician mapping warmer ice near a doorway, or a Yosemite ranger comparing sunlit granite and shaded trees. These are proposed uses for your detective team, not claims that a particular rink or park uses this system. A suitable camera, safe access and real measurements would be needed.

04 · Experiment

Can you hide warmth with a sleeve?

Materials: two identical plastic cups, comfortably warm tap water (not hot), a towel, a kitchen thermometer, paper and pencil. Ask an adult to check water temperature and help with spills.

  1. Put equal amounts of the same warm water into both cups. Measure and record the starting temperatures. Wrap a dry towel around the sides of one cup; keep the other bare. Keep the tops equally uncovered.
  2. Place both on the same counter, away from sunlight and drafts. Predict which water will cool faster.
  3. Measure both at 5 and 10 minutes with the same thermometer. Wait for a stable reading, stir gently in the same way, and avoid touching the cup wall with the probe.
  4. Calculate the temperature drop from the start. Repeat once with cups swapped to check whether location affected the result.

A towel usually slows heat transfer through the sides, but evaporation through the open tops and measurement uncertainty matter. This measures water temperature, not infrared radiation. If an adult has a thermal camera, separately compare the outside surfaces; don't expect the outside towel temperature to equal the water temperature.

05 · Code

Teach a computer to color numbers

Our page uses plain JavaScript. This small version of the lab's color algorithm divides a range into five bands and keeps numbers outside the range in the nearest end band. A constant range gets the middle color.

function band(value, lo, hi) {
  if (hi === lo) return 2;
  return Math.min(4, Math.max(0,
    Math.floor(5 * (value - lo) / (hi - lo))));
}

console.log(band(32, 10, 50)); // 2
console.log(band(32, 18, 32)); // 4

The same 32 °C gets different color numbers with different scales! The algorithm expects finite numbers and an ordered range. Try it with a grown-up in a browser console. No installation, account, library, network or camera permission is needed.

06 · Connect

From the hockey rink to spacecraft

Hockey lab

Goalies wear thick padding and skaters wear layered gear. Insulation can slow heat loss. The same idea helps explain your towel experiment; it does not mean every pad has the same temperature.

Dad's GRACE-C mission

GRACE-C is planned to measure tiny gravity changes to learn where Earth's water and ice mass move. A thermal instrument measures infrared light instead. Both turn invisible clues into maps, but GRACE-C is not a heat camera. NASA mission page.

SharpHDL and VeloSim

Imagine designing a tiny digital circuit that sorts temperature readings into five color bins. SharpHDL is about describing digital hardware and VeloSim is about simulating it. You could test the boundaries before making a real circuit. This is an idea, not a claim these projects operate a NASA camera.

What can go wrong?

Clouds can block a view of the ground. A pixel may mix several surfaces. Shiny materials can reflect surrounding infrared. Calibration errors and the atmosphere can change the estimate. Thermal maps are useful clues, but they need context, clear legends and checks against independent measurements.

07 · Check yourself

Five questions for thermal detectives

1. Are the red areas in a thermal image really glowing red to our eyes?

No. The map assigns visible colors to measured or estimated values.

2. Does changing the color scale change the temperature?

No. It changes how the number is drawn.

3. Can a 100-meter thermal pixel measure one hockey puck?

No. Its footprint covers a much larger area.

4. Why might a shiny object fool a camera?

It can reflect infrared radiation from its surroundings.

5. Does GRACE-C measure heat in the same way as Landsat?

No. GRACE-C is designed to measure changes in Earth's gravity field; Landsat TIRS detects thermal infrared radiation.

Mission complete: You have learned that a picture can be a scientific measurement, but its colors are not the measurement itself.
