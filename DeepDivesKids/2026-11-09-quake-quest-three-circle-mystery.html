01 · The case

The island heard a rumble … but from where?

Picture three tiny earthquake detectors named Ada, Byte, and Comet. Each writes down the instant a speedy P wave arrives and the instant a slower S wave follows.

Station Ada might say: “I heard the first wave … then the next wave 20 seconds later!” That tells us the quake was a certain distance away. But was it north, south, east, or west? Ada cannot know from that clue alone.

🔍 Detective trick: Draw a circle around Ada. Repeat around Byte and Comet. The spot where all three circles cross is your best answer in our model.

And yes, in real life, scientists do use seismometers and arrival times to locate earthquakes. The three-circle classroom trick is a simplified version of that work.

02 · How it works

Two waves. One stopwatch clue.

Feel the fast one

P stands for primary. In solid rock, these waves squeeze and stretch the material in their direction of travel.

Wait for the wiggle

S stands for secondary. These waves shake material sideways and arrive after P waves.

Find the delay

Subtract the P-wave arrival time from the S-wave arrival time. A bigger gap usually means more distance.

Our easy-mode model: P waves always move at 6 kilometers per second, and S waves at 3.5 kilometers per second. The pretend island is flat, and the quake starts at its surface. Real Earth is not this simple! These fixed speeds are illustrative assumptions, not measured values for a particular place.

In this model, distance = (S time − P time) ÷ (1/3.5 − 1/6). That funny number works because the slower S wave needs more time for the same journey.

Quick challenge: If the S wave arrives 10 seconds after the P wave, the station is 84 km from the quake in our pretend world. Try another gap in the lab!

Why isn't one station enough?

It tells you how far away the earthquake was, but not which direction. Imagine standing on a hockey rink and hearing a whistle from exactly 15 meters away: the whistle could be anywhere on a circle around you. A second station narrows the choices; a third usually picks the crossing point.

05 · Do it yourself

📐 The paper-and-string earthquake hunt

You need: a sheet of paper, pencil, ruler, a piece of string, and a helper (optional). No shaking equipment required.

  1. Draw a rectangle 12 cm wide and 7.6 cm tall. That is our 600 km × 380 km island: 1 cm = 50 km.
  2. Mark three stations measured from the top-left corner: Ada (2.2 cm right, 1.8 cm down), Byte (9.7 cm right, 2.1 cm down), and Comet (6.0 cm right, 6.4 cm down).
  3. Choose Case 1. Look at the game's “Circle radius” column, divide each radius in kilometers by 50, and set that length on your ruler or string.
  4. Hold one end of the string at Ada and swing the pencil around to make a circle. Repeat at Byte and Comet using their radii.
  5. Find the place where all the circles cross. Compare with your online guess. What happened when you tried just one circle?

Bonus for two detectives: One person draws the circles; the other checks the result in the web lab. Swap jobs for Case 2.

Rounding the on-screen times to tenths of a second makes pencil circles approximate. That's fine—real science also needs to talk about measurement uncertainty.

06 · The code behind the clue

💻 A tiny bit of JavaScript detective math

The same little formula runs inside this page. It does not need a special library, account, server, or internet connection after you save the file.

// Speed assumptions for our imaginary island:
const pSpeed = 6;    // km per second
const sSpeed = 3.5;  // km per second

function distanceFromGap(seconds) {
  return seconds / (1 / sSpeed - 1 / pSpeed);
}

console.log(distanceFromGap(10)); // 84 kilometers

Try changing 10 to 5. Would the earthquake be closer or farther away? In this model, half the delay means half the distance.

How engineers use the same idea: Your dad's RISCue software handles events with careful timing. A seismic station also records events—in this case, when a wave arrives. VeloSim explores repeatable simulations. Our three pretend quakes are repeatable too: the same station positions and wave speeds give the same answers every time. These are learning connections, not claims that either project processes earthquake data.

Hockey connection: Two teammates who hear a slap shot at different times have timing clues. Like the goalie reaction-time game, timing matters! But sound in an ice rink is not the same as seismic waves traveling through rock.

07 · Scientist's honesty badge

What this model cannot tell you

🚨 Not a warning tool: Never use this page to judge earthquake safety, predict an earthquake, or decide whether to evacuate. Follow official local guidance. Earthquakes cannot currently be predicted by date, place, and size.

🪨 Rock isn't uniform

Real seismic waves speed up, slow down, and bend through different materials. Scientists use travel-time models, not our two fixed speeds.

📍 Quakes have depth

Earthquakes start underground at a hypocenter. The epicenter is the point on the surface above it. Our island pretends the source is right at the surface.

⏱️ Measurements are messy

Real sensors have uncertain arrival times. More stations, calibration, and computer calculations improve location estimates. Three drawn circles are a teaching tool.

Explorer quiz: Which wave reaches a station first?

The P wave! It travels faster than the S wave in our model and generally arrives first in real seismic records.

Explorer quiz: What is an epicenter?

The spot on Earth's surface above where an earthquake starts underground. The underground starting point is called the hypocenter.

Explorer quiz: Can this game predict the next earthquake?

No. It locates an already occurred, imaginary earthquake using pretend arrival data. Real ShakeAlert is an early warning system after a quake begins, not a prediction system.
