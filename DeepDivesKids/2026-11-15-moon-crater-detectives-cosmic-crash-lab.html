01 / Look up!

🌙 Why is the Moon covered in circles?

Imagine an old sidewalk with footprints frozen in it. The Moon is a bit like that, but its marks are impact craters: dents left by space rocks colliding with the ground. Because the Moon has almost no atmosphere and little weather or flowing water, many impact marks remain for an incredibly long time.

☄️ The rockA space rock, called a meteoroid before it hits, races toward the surface.
💥 The crashIts motion energy moves and breaks the ground. Material flies outward.
⭕ The clueA round crater, raised rim, and sometimes bright rays remain.
Detective question: Do you think a heavier marble dropped from the same height will make exactly the same dent as a lighter marble? Write down your prediction before trying the lab.

02 / Know the science

⚡ Motion energy: the crash's secret ingredient

Objects gain speed as they fall. A ball held higher above the ground has more energy waiting to turn into motion when released. In our simple drop experiment, the energy just before impact is:

Energy = mass × gravity × drop height
E = m × g × h (joules, with kilograms and meters)

This is the gravitational potential energy before the drop. If we ignore air resistance, it becomes motion (kinetic) energy just before the hit.

Our units are tiny!

A 20-gram marble is 0.020 kilograms. A 50-centimeter drop is 0.50 meters.

On Earth: 0.020 × 9.81 × 0.50 = 0.0981 joules, also called 98.1 millijoules (mJ).

On the Moon, gravity is about one-sixth Earth's, but actual lunar meteoroids can hit at enormous speeds. Our gentle marble drops are not miniature copies of those crashes.

03 / Evidence from the real world

🔬 NASA actually does crater detective work

Documented real use Spacecraft spot new craters

NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed the lunar surface before and after an impact, revealing a new crater about 12 meters across and streaks of scattered material. Scientists also count craters to help estimate how old a lunar surface is. NASA: Why Study Moon Craters?

Documented real use Telescopes watch for flashes

NASA's Meteoroid Environment Office uses two video-equipped telescopes to look for brief flashes when rocks hit the Moon. Seeing the same flash in both helps scientists rule out camera noise or a cosmic ray striking just one sensor. NASA: Lunar Impact Monitoring

And yes, NASA makes impact craters in a laboratory! NASA describes using its Vertical Gun Range to shoot small projectiles at high speed into target materials. That helps scientists study what real, very fast impacts do. Our marble-and-flour activity is only a safe, gentle classroom analogy. See NASA's explanation.

05 / Unplug and explore

🥣 Make real craters with flour!

Gather: a shallow tray, flour, a little cocoa powder for contrast, one marble or small ball, a ruler, scrap paper, and an adult helper. Put newspaper underneath for easy cleanup.

  1. Spread 2–3 cm of flour in the tray and dust the top with cocoa. Smooth the surface.
  2. Hold the same marble 10 cm above the surface and release it without pushing. Measure the widest part of its crater with the ruler.
  3. Smooth the flour. Repeat from 30 cm and 50 cm, using the same marble and landing position.
  4. Record the crater diameter each time. For a fair test, change just one thing — the height.
  5. Try the same drop height with a second, differently sized ball. Predict first, then compare.
🧠 Prediction: A higher drop usually releases more energy. But the crater's width is NOT simply proportional to energy; your measurements may vary because flour packing, the ball, and the way it lands all matter.

Safety: An adult should supervise small marbles and powder. Do not throw rocks or aim anything at people, animals, windows, or screens. Avoid breathing flour or cocoa dust; wash hands afterward and do not eat the experiment.

On paper: Draw three circles with the actual crater diameters you measured. Give your graph a title, label the heights, and write one sentence about whether your prediction matched what happened.

06 / Code corner

💻 You're programming a science calculation!

Our live lab uses built-in JavaScript — no download or extra library. This is the core calculation, shown as a small piece of real working code:

function dropEnergy(massGrams, heightCm, gravity) {
  const massKg = massGrams / 1000;
  const heightM = heightCm / 100;
  const joules = massKg * gravity * heightM;
  const speedMps = Math.sqrt(2 * gravity * heightM);
  return { joules, speedMps };
}

// A 20 g marble dropped 50 cm on Earth:
const result = dropEnergy(20, 50, 9.81);
console.log(result.joules);   // 0.0981 joules
console.log(result.speedMps); // about 3.13 m/s

Try a detective trick: change only 20 to 40. You'll double the energy, but the speed stays the same, because in this simplified model the speed depends on height and gravity — not mass.

Why no library this time? The browser already supports range sliders, drawing with SVG, and JavaScript math. Adding a big coding library would make this little experiment harder to save and run offline. Learn more at MDN's official Math.sqrt reference.

08 / Honest science

🧪 What's real? What's pretend?

✅ Real and checkable

NASA photographs craters, watches for lunar impact flashes, and uses high-speed experiments to study impacts. Our code correctly calculates idealized falling-object energy from mass, gravity, and height.

🎨 Simplified or imagined

The cartoon Moon, drawn crater sizes, trial labels, and mission story are made up. The lab is not a NASA crater simulator. It ignores air resistance, rock breaking, surface composition, and most of the physics of high-speed meteor impacts.

The Moon setting is a thought experiment for dropping a marble from rest in lunar gravity. It is not the energy of a meteoroid arriving from space. A real meteoroid usually arrives vastly faster than a dropped marble.

09 / Bring it home

🥅 Hockey, space, and Dad's computer projects

For Quinn and Reagan

Think of a hockey puck: a slow, soft tap and a booming slap shot do not have the same impact energy. Goalie gear is designed to help protect against collisions. Our crater activity explores how impacts can change a surface, but it's not a test of hockey safety equipment.

When you visit new places, look for circular landforms or rocks, but remember: not every round hole is a meteor crater! Scientists check many clues.

For Dad's engineering lab

Dad's VeloSim and SharpHDL projects work with computer models and hardware logic. Your lab has a similar engineering habit: keep inputs clear, calculate results repeatably, and test whether changing one input gives an expected result. In engineering that is called verification.

Mini challenge: Set Earth, 20 g, 50 cm; record energy. Press Drop twice without moving a slider. Do you see the same number both times?
