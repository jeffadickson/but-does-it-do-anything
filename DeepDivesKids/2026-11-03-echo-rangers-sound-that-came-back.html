🎯 Mission: Find the invisible wall

Imagine standing in a huge, dark ice rink. You hear a sharp CLAP! and then — a quieter clap. That second sound is an echo. Sound traveled to a surface, bounced, and came back.

🧠 The secret: The sound takes a round trip

  1. Send a ping. A vibration travels through air or water as a sound wave.
  2. Meet an object. Some of the wave reflects off a wall, insect, or seafloor.
  3. Listen for the echo. The reflection travels back to the listener.
  4. Do the detective math. Time tells you the total travel distance. Divide by two to find the one-way distance.
Distance = (speed × echo time) ÷ 2

Why ÷ 2? The ping went there and came back.

Try the 17-meter riddle. In our simple air model, sound travels at 340 meters per second. To get to a wall 17 meters away and return, it travels 34 meters. The round trip takes 34 ÷ 340 = 0.10 seconds = 100 milliseconds. One millisecond is one-thousandth of a second.

Sound travels at different speeds in different materials. NOAA gives useful rounded examples: about 340 meters/second in air and 1,500 meters/second in seawater. Real speed varies with conditions — especially temperature in air and temperature, pressure, and saltiness in seawater. Read NOAA's explanation.

🌎 This is real — not just a video-game trick

DOCUMENTED REAL USE

🦇 Bats hunting insects

The U.S. National Park Service explains that bats make high-frequency calls and listen for returning echoes to locate objects and prey. Some bats speed up their calls as they close in on a snack. The calls often use ultrasound, too high-pitched for people to hear.

National Park Service: Echolocation

DOCUMENTED REAL USE

🚢 Ships mapping the ocean

NOAA ships use sonar equipment that sends sound down toward the seafloor and listens for echoes. The return time helps researchers calculate depth. Some sonar systems send many beams to create maps of underwater terrain.

NOAA: What is sonar? · NOAA: Seafloor echoes

🧰 Try it in the real world

Paper detective: The double journey

You need: paper, a pencil, a ruler, and an optional calculator.

  1. Draw yourself and a pretend cave wall 17 boxes apart.
  2. Draw 17 arrows toward the wall, then 17 back to yourself.
  3. Count all 34 boxes. That is the sound's total trip.
  4. Calculate 34 ÷ 340 = 0.1 second. Why does the one-way distance stay 17?

Extension: If an echo takes 0.2 second in air, what is the one-way distance? (Check your answer below.)

Reveal the extension answer

340 × 0.2 ÷ 2 = 34 meters.

Listen for an echo (with an adult)

  1. Find a safe, open, quiet place with a distant hard wall, such as a large gym. Ask permission first.
  2. Clap once, then listen. Try moving farther away and compare what you hear.
  3. Explain why a nearer wall's echo can blend into the original clap.

Safety: No shouting near other people's ears, climbing, or entering restricted spaces. You may not hear a clear echo, and that is normal: rooms absorb and scatter sound.

Important: A hand stopwatch cannot reliably measure a 100-millisecond echo. Use the computer lab for the numbers, and your ears for the experience.

💻 Meet the tiny program inside the lab

Every time the sliders change, JavaScript uses one simple calculation. The program stores speed in meters per second, distance in meters, and converts seconds into milliseconds for display.

// The echo travels to the wall AND back.
function echoTimeMs(distanceMeters, speedMetersPerSecond) {
  return (2 * distanceMeters / speedMetersPerSecond) * 1000;
}

function distanceFromEcho(timeMs, speedMetersPerSecond) {
  return (timeMs / 1000) * speedMetersPerSecond / 2;
}

// Open air, 17-meter wall:
echoTimeMs(17, 340);        // 100 milliseconds
distanceFromEcho(100, 340); // 17 meters

// Seawater, 30-meter wall:
echoTimeMs(30, 1500);       // 40 milliseconds

Computer-science idea: The simulation separates the model (a math equation) from the picture (an animated dot). That means the animation can be slowed down without changing the real calculated echo time.

REAL WORLD PRINCIPLE

Ships and scientists

Echo timing is used in sonar. NOAA documents how real survey equipment uses reflected sound to measure seafloor depth. Your lab uses the same core idea, but the ocean's physics and real instruments are much more complicated.

🧭 A good scientist notices what the model misses

What our model gets right

A reflected wave must travel out and back. Distance depends on wave speed and return time. Sound is much faster in seawater than in air, using NOAA's rounded example speeds.

What our model leaves out

Real echoes may be quiet, delayed by many obstacles, scattered, or not detected at all. Weather, water conditions, surface shape, direction, motion, and equipment accuracy matter. Bats also interpret complex sound patterns, not just one perfect wall echo.

Careful! Sound is not the same as radar or laser light. They can all use travel time to estimate distance, but radar and lidar use electromagnetic waves rather than sound waves. This lesson is about sound.
