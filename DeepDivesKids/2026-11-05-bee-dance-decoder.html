01 · Big question

What if your teammate could give directions without talking?

Imagine Quinn spots a perfect empty space near the hockey net. He cannot shout, so he skates in a tiny pattern to tell Reagan where to look. That sounds like a game—but for honey bees, something like this actually happens!

A honey bee visiting flowers can return to the hive and do a waggle dance. Other bees follow her movements and gather clues about where a rewarding flower patch is. The dance is information, not just celebration. [1] [2]

Science fact: the researchers study real bees, not just cartoon ones. In 1973 Karl von Frisch shared a Nobel Prize for research including how bees communicate. [1]

02 · Nature’s navigation code

A figure eight with two secret clues

Clue A · Direction

Look at the tilt

On a vertical honeycomb, running straight up during the waggle means “fly toward the Sun.” Tilting 30° right of up means “fly 30° right of the Sun’s direction.” Tilting left works too. Real bees can dance inside a dark hive. [1]

Clue B · Distance

Watch how long she waggles

A longer waggle run generally reports a farther food source. This is a clue about travel distance, not a precise stopwatch-to-meters formula for every bee or landscape. [3]

The return loops

After the straight waggle, the bee turns back around and repeats. The repeated paths form something like a figure eight. The straight middle section carries the key angle and duration information. [4]

The Sun moves!

As the Sun appears to cross the sky, bees can adjust directions. A bee’s dance isn’t a fixed compass pointing north. This lesson places the Sun at the top on purpose so the geometry is easy to see. [1]

Try this: point one arm straight ahead to represent the Sun. Rotate your other arm 45° to the right. That’s the kind of angle the bee is communicating. You do not have to know which way north is!

03 · Evidence vs. ideas

Scientists really decode these dances

Verified research · 2024

Reading a landscape through bees

Researchers decoded dances from 20 honey bee colonies in urban and rural parts of southeast England to investigate where bees traveled for food. These are actual analyzed dances, not a made-up use of the technique. [5]

Verified research · 2023

Young bees learn from experts

UC San Diego researchers and collaborators found that young bees that could not watch experienced dancers made less accurate dances. Practice and learning matter to these insects! [4]

Verified research · 2026

The audience matters

A study published March 23, 2026 found that a scout’s dance directions became less precise when fewer followers watched. Bees even adjust to their audience. [3]

Imagined game · not a real device

Could a robot dance?

We could program a pretend swarm of classroom robots to share directions using angles and timing, like our browser toy below. That is our design idea, not evidence that Dad’s RISCue or VeloSim already uses bee dances.

05 · Try it for real (without bees!)

A paper dance-code treasure hunt

  1. Draw a big circle on paper and mark UP = Sun at the top. Put a paper “hive” in the middle.
  2. Hide a pretend flower (a sticker or small drawing) somewhere on the page.
  3. One child plays the scout: point a pencil up, then turn it toward the hidden flower. Count an approximate angle (0°, 45°, or 90° works great).
  4. Choose a short, medium, or long waggle to show whether it is near, in between, or far in your paper world.
  5. The other child draws the flight. How close can they get? Switch roles!

For an extra challenge: spin the paper “Sun” marker to a different direction and encode the same flower again. A new reference direction needs a new angle.

Safety: no need to touch, trap, or disturb actual bees. If you watch real bees outdoors, give them space and ask an adult about insect allergies.

06 · Code explorer

How a tiny bit of math draws the route

The web page uses the browser’s built-in JavaScript + SVG drawing tools. The angle is measured from up (toward the pretend Sun), and the length comes from the chosen waggle setting.

const degrees = 30;                 // right of the Sun
const length = 70;                   // picture pixels, NOT meters
const radians = degrees * Math.PI / 180;
const x = 180 + Math.sin(radians) * length;
const y = 220 - Math.cos(radians) * length;
// SVG coordinates grow DOWN, so "up" subtracts y.
// x and y mark our pretend flower's position.

At 0°, Math.sin(0) is 0, so the destination stays directly above the hive. Try 90°: Math.cos(90°) is about 0, so the arrow points right instead.

Why can this work without a coding library?

Browsers already know how to draw SVG shapes and respond to slider and button events. This lesson adds no external library, downloads, fonts, photographs, or network requests. Save this HTML file, then double-click it to run it offline in a modern browser. A future classroom version could use p5.js—but we already explored p5.js in an earlier lesson, so this one uses native browser features.

07 · Your world

Bees, hockey, and Dad’s engineering projects

🏒 Quinn & Reagan: teamwork

On the ice, a glance or a stick point can tell a teammate where to go. A goalie and defenders also share information quickly. Bee dances are a nature example of passing location clues—not a hockey playbook.

🧰 RISCue: messages

In Dad’s RISCue RTOS work, a computer task can send information to another task. Imagine a message with two fields: direction and distance. Bees inspire the analogy; this is a suggested classroom design, not an implemented RISCue feature.

🧪 VeloSim: experiments

With a simulation, you can repeat the same starting position and inputs to check whether you get the same result. Our little map behaves that way too: same angle + length = same drawn location. Actual bee flights have wind, landmarks, and uncertainty!

🌻 Nature detectives

A healthy habitat offers flowers across the seasons. Scientists can decode waggle dances to help understand how colonies use landscapes. The research is real; our pretend flower patches are just a learning game. [5]

08 · Science needs careful thinking

What our colorful toy does not show

  • No exact mile counter. Our “quick / middle / long” labels are invented for teaching. In real hives, dance timing and distance depend on biology and conditions; our lengths are screen pixels, not meters.
  • Not all bees dance like this. This lesson is about honey bees; it is not a rule for bumble bees or every kind of bee. [2]
  • Not a perfect GPS. Bees also use visual and other sensory cues. Dance directions can be noisy, and even change with the audience. [3]
  • Not a real hive experiment. The figure eight and flower field are original schematic artwork. We did not collect bee observations or validate this toy against recorded waggle dances.

Science challenge: What if the scout’s directions were off by 15°? What if every follower made a slightly different guess? Where should a robot search next? This is a great way to explore uncertainty without pretending our model is nature itself.
