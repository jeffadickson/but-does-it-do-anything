01 / The mystery

How can a heavy boat stay on top of water?

Quinn and Reagan are preparing a make-believe lake expedition: science instruments, snacks, and hockey pucks need to reach a camp across the water. Their boat is strong—but cargo is heavy. Add too much, and water comes over the rim. How can they decide before launching how much it can carry?

Make a prediction: Will a wide boat or a narrow boat hold more boxes if both have the same rim height? Why might a ship float while a small metal coin sinks?

02 / The science

Three secrets of floating

1 Water moves aside

A boat pushes some water out of the space it occupies. Scientists call this displacement. More underwater volume means more water pushed aside.

2 Water pushes up

Water pressure pushes on the boat. The buoyant force equals the weight of the water displaced. That is Archimedes' principle.

3 Find the balance

A floating boat settles until its downward weight matches that upward force. Add cargo and it sits deeper. Let water pour over the rim and things get messy!

Surprise: A huge steel ship can float because its hollow shape includes lots of air and lets it move aside enough water. It is not because steel itself is lighter than water. NOAA explains the idea and demonstrates foil boats.

One more clue: salty water is a little denser than fresh water, so the same boat usually floats a bit higher in the ocean. The USGS Water Science School explains density and why dissolved salt changes it.

03 / Real engineering, checked against sources

People really use this science!

Documented real use

🚢 Cargo ships and busy ports

NOAA explains that extra cargo makes a ship sit deeper. Its draft is the depth of ship below the waterline, and port teams need accurate water-depth information to keep ships from hitting the bottom.

Source: NOAA National Ocean Service, port navigation and draft

Documented real use

🤖 Argo's ocean robots

Real Argo floats move oil into an outside bladder to increase their volume without changing their mass much. That helps them rise. Pumping oil back inside helps them sink. Onboard computers guide the journey and sensors measure the ocean.

Source: International Argo Program, how floats work

Model rules, not a real launch certificate: We imagine a watertight, vertical-sided box. The dry mass is 60 + (length × width ÷ 4) grams; each crate is 120 g; water density is fixed to 1.000 or 1.025 g/cm³. Safe means no more than 80% of hull height is underwater. We ignore tipping, waves, water temperature, hull thickness, leaks, and changes in load balance. The actual waterline of a homemade foil boat will differ.

05 / Take it off the screen

The aluminum-foil shipyard

🔬 Your supplies

Two equally sized pieces of aluminum foil, a bowl or tub of water, a towel, 15–30 small paper clips or pennies, paper, and a pencil. Ask an adult to help with water and any sharp foil edges.

  1. Fold a narrow boat from the first sheet. Fold a wide boat from the second.
  2. Predict which will carry more coins. Write down your guess.
  3. Float each boat, then place weights near the center, one at a time.
  4. Stop when the boat tips or water reaches the rim. Record the last successful count.
  5. Change just one thing and try again. Does a taller rim help? How about moving coins to one side?

06 / Peek inside the code

Computers can test ideas before we build them

The boat simulation is written in plain JavaScript, the same language web pages use. No library, app install, account, or internet connection is needed once you have saved this HTML page.

Here is the core idea, using the same formula as the working lab:

// Length, width, height: centimeters
// Mass: grams; water density: grams per cubic centimeter
const hullMass = 60 + (length * width / 4);
const totalMass = hullMass + crates * 120;
const density = water === "salt" ? 1.025 : 1.000;
const displacedVolume = totalMass / density;
const draft = displacedVolume / (length * width);
const safe = draft <= 0.8 * height;
// A real boat also needs stability and strength checks.

What happens? Divide the boat's mass by water density to find how many cubic centimeters of water must be displaced. Divide by the boat's flat bottom area to find depth underwater. One square centimeter times one centimeter equals one cubic centimeter.

Dad's engineering connection: With RISCue and VeloSim, an engineer checks how a system behaves step by step before trusting hardware. Our boat lab does the same kind of experiment: change one input, calculate a predictable result, and see whether a safety rule passes. This isn't actual RISCue or VeloSim software—just the same engineering habit.

Bonus brain teaser: Can a boat float but still be unsafe?

Yes! It might tip, leak, break, or have almost no rim left above the water. That's why real engineers check more than just whether an object floats.

08 / Engineer's honest notebook

What our game gets right—and what it can't

✅ Useful ideas

It models weight balanced by displaced-water weight, uses explicit measurement units, shows added cargo increasing draft, and shows the small density difference between fresh and salty water.

⚠️ Simplifications

Actual hulls curve; foil bends; cargo shifts; boats tip; wind and waves act on them. Our rectangular boat, fixed toy weights, and 20% margin are invented teaching choices, not safety standards.
