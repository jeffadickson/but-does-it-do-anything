The mystery

Could a tiny push cause a giant wiggle?

Imagine gently pushing a playground swing. Push it at the right moments, and it travels farther and farther without needing one enormous shove.

But if you push randomly, sometimes you help and sometimes you fight against its motion. The swing has a favorite back-and-forth rhythm, called its natural frequency. Matching that rhythm can build up a bigger response. That is resonance. [1]

One more character joins the story: damping. It takes energy away, like friction and air drag, and helps stop the wiggle. [2]

🎵 Three secrets of shaking

1. Rhythm

Frequency

How many complete back-and-forth trips happen in one second? One trip each second is 1 hertz (Hz).

2. Response

Resonance

When an outside push follows the object's preferred rhythm, the wiggle can become much larger. The largest response is usually near the natural frequency.

3. Brakes

Damping

Friction and other effects remove motion's energy. More damping usually makes the resonance peak smaller and wider.

Engineer clue: A small push is not magic free energy. Repeated pushes add energy over time. Damping carries some of that energy away. Real things do not wiggle without limit.

🔍 Curious about the math? (Grown-up bonus)

This is the steady-state, linear, single-degree-of-freedom force-driven oscillator. With r = push frequency / natural frequency and damping ratio ζ, the dimensionless dynamic magnification is:

Shake factor = 1 / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²).

It compares oscillation amplitude to displacement under the same force held steady, not to the size of the drawing. The model assumes sinusoidal forcing, one vibration mode, linear stiffness, and viscous damping. Formula source: OpenStax [1]. The graph shows the steady response after the initial transient dies away.

🛰️ Engineers actually shake spacecraft!

Verified NASA example

Perseverance's loud rehearsal

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory tested the spacecraft carrying the Perseverance Mars rover with very loud sound before launch. NASA reported noise as high as about 143 decibels during acoustic testing. Engineers inspected for loose or damaged parts; some fasteners were tightened and some cables replaced. [3]

Important: loud sound testing is not the same as our single-frequency spring game. Launch sound creates complicated vibrations.

Verified ESA example

HYDRA, the giant shaking table

The European Space Agency's HYDRA facility moves spacecraft test articles along several axes to imitate launch vibration. ESA describes tests that search for troublesome resonant responses; its platform is about 5.5 by 5.5 meters across. [4]

That is a real specialized machine run by experts. Our online spring is just a safe learning model.

Verified NASA practice

Engineers find the hidden beats

NASA Glenn's Structural Dynamics Laboratory performs vibration testing and measures resonant frequencies, damping, and vibration patterns. The results help engineers check whether models agree with real hardware. [5]

Evidence versus imagination: NASA and ESA truly perform vibration tests described above. Our cartoon rocket, its 1.20 Hz favorite rhythm, and its damping choices are invented teaching examples. We did not measure the resonance of a real rover, rocket, hockey stick, or bridge.

🧪 Kitchen-table experiment: the wiggling ruler

Supplies: a flexible plastic ruler, a heavy book, paper, pencil, and an adult to help arrange the book. Do not use glass or a brittle ruler.

  1. Put a ruler on a sturdy table and hold it down with the book. Let about 12 cm stick past the edge.
  2. Gently bend the free tip down a little, release, and watch it wiggle. Count how many wiggles you see in about five seconds. Do not pull it far or snap it.
  3. Move the book so only about 6 cm of ruler sticks out. Try the same gentle release and count again.
  4. Write your result. Prediction: will the shorter free end usually wiggle faster or slower? Why?
  5. For an extra challenge, try a very small rhythmic tap with one fingertip. Does the timing matter? Stop if the ruler begins to bend too far.

🏒 From the rink to Dad's engineering desk

On the ice

After a hockey stick hits a puck, the stick can bend and vibrate. The connection is that sports gear, too, has stiffness and damping. This lesson does not calculate a particular stick's response or recommend changing anyone's equipment.

Goalies also care about absorbing impacts. A goalie pad is not our simple spring, but it gives you a reason to ask: Where does the energy go?

In software and space

Engineers often write a model — a computer description of how something should behave — and test whether the model agrees with reality.

Projects like VeloSim and SharpHDL are part of Dad's engineering-programming world. They concern electronic designs and simulation, not this particular vibration calculation. The shared idea is predict, test, find mistakes, improve.

⚖️ Good engineers admit what their model cannot do

  • One spring, not an entire rocket: real spacecraft can bend in many complicated patterns (engineers call them vibration modes).
  • Smooth rhythm, not a noisy launch: this lab applies one repeating push. Real launches have random vibration, multiple directions, and very loud sound.
  • Simple damping: materials, joints, and bolts do not always behave like our easy settings.
  • Steady-state, not startup: the formula describes the response after initial motion settles. The animation illustrates that response, not a full launch history.
  • Not a safety test: no real-world failure load, allowable motion, structural stress, or launch qualification can be inferred from the picture or the shake factor.

Why use it, then? Because a simple model can teach a powerful question: What happens if the push arrives at just the wrong rhythm? Then engineers build richer models and carefully test the hardware.

🧮 The important JavaScript formula 
function shakeFactor(pushHz, naturalHz, damping) {
  const r = pushHz / naturalHz;
  return 1 / Math.sqrt(
    Math.pow(1 - r * r, 2) +
    Math.pow(2 * damping * r, 2)
  );
}
// shakeFactor(1.2, 1.2, 0.08) is 6.25.

Try the formula in a JavaScript console, or change the sliders to calculate it here. The full lab's JavaScript is included at the end of this page.
