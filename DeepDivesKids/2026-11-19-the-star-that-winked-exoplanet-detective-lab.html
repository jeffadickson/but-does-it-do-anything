The case of the disappearing light

What if you found a planet without ever seeing it?

Imagine standing a million miles from a lighthouse. A tiny moth flies across its bright lamp. You probably cannot see the moth — but with a very sensitive light meter, you might notice the lighthouse dim for a moment.

Astronomers use a similar clue. When a planet moves between its star and a telescope, it blocks some starlight. The telescope records a tiny dip. When the dips happen again and again on a regular schedule, that's exciting evidence that a planet might be orbiting the star.

Mission: make a pretend star, invent a planet, and figure out how its size changes the dip. Then check a real NASA discovery.

🔭 How the light trick works

1Watch

Measure a star's light again and again. A spacecraft like NASA's TESS does this with cameras.

2Notice

Plot the light measurements. A small dip can happen when a planet crosses in front of the star. That's called a transit.

3Check

Watch for repeat dips, rule out other explanations, and use more observations. A dip is a clue, not proof by itself.

Vocabulary power-up

Light curve

A graph of how bright something appears over time. A planet crossing a star makes a little valley, like a tiny smile turned upside down.

In our lab, the bottom axis shows position across the star, not real clock time. We hold everything else steady.

Secret math

Why big planets make deeper dips

For a planet completely in front of a uniformly bright star, the blocked fraction is approximately:

(planet radius ÷ star radius)²

So a planet with 10% of the star's radius can hide about 1% of its light. Double the planet's radius to 20%, and it hides about 4%, not 2%!

🌟 Three real things space scientists did

Verified NASA + MIT mission

TESS watches stars

Launched April 18, 2018, NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite uses four cameras to monitor enormous parts of the sky. It looks for repeatable changes in starlight that suggest transiting planets.

Primary sources: NASA TESS mission; MIT TESS mission overview.

Verified 2020 discovery

A world called TOI 700 d

On January 7, 2020, NASA described TOI 700 d, TESS's first discovered Earth-size planet in a star's habitable zone — a distance where liquid water might be possible under suitable conditions.

Primary source: NASA announcement. A habitable zone is not evidence of life.

Verified JPL 2023 discovery

Then they found planet e!

On January 10, 2023, NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory reported another Earth-size world, TOI 700 e. Scientists needed an extra year of TESS observations to detect its much fainter repeating signal.

Primary source: NASA/JPL report.

Evidence vs. imagination: don't mix them up

✅ Observed and supported: TESS measures changing starlight. Scientists have identified transiting planets, including TOI 700 d and e, using observations and checks.

✅ Mathematical model: our dark circle blocks a calculable area of a bright circle.

💭 Invented for play: the colored star, little blue planet, graph settings, and smooth noiseless readings in the lab. They are not TESS data.

❓ Still unknown: whether life exists on TOI 700 d or e, and exactly what their surfaces are like.

🔦 Kitchen-table experiment: eclipse a flashlight

Materials: LED flashlight, white sheet of paper, a coin or small cardboard circle, tape, a pencil, and a helpful grown-up. No special app needed.

  1. In a slightly dark room, shine the LED flashlight onto a white wall or another sheet of paper. Never stare into the flashlight.
  2. Tape a cardboard circle to a pencil so you can hold it by the handle. Move it across the flashlight beam between the light and the wall.
  3. Observe how the bright patch changes. What happens with a larger cardboard circle? What happens when the circle moves out of the beam?
  4. Draw a simple brightness-versus-position graph. Use words like bright, dimmer, and bright rather than pretending your eyes can measure precise percentages.

Important: this is a classroom analogy, not a true telescope measurement. The wall can show a shadow, the flashlight beam is uneven, and your eyes are not calibrated light meters. Our computer model is a cleaner demonstration of the geometry.

Extra experiment: Can you trick the detector?

Have a friend make the flashlight flicker or partly cover it with a different object. Your observer might see a dip — but the dip doesn't prove a planet! Scientists must test alternative explanations before naming a planet.

Bonus: Why doesn't TESS find every planet?

The orbit must line up so a planet passes in front of its star from our viewpoint. Many planets never cross their stars as we see them. Tiny dips also get lost among measurement noise and a star's own changing brightness.

⚖️ Good scientists know the limits

What our model gets right

A round dark planet can block part of a round bright star. More overlap means less light. A fully centered planet of 10% stellar radius hides about 1% of the star's light, in our uniformly bright-star model.

What our model skips

Real stars are not equally bright across their faces (called limb darkening). Real light curves contain noise and starspots; planets take time to cross and orbit; multiple stars can imitate some signals. Astronomers fit far more careful models and seek confirming evidence.

One more caution: a transit by itself mainly helps estimate planet-to-star size ratio; knowing actual size accurately requires information about the star too. It does not automatically tell us the planet's mass, air, water, or life.

🏒 What this has to do with hockey and engineering

On the ice: spot the tiny clue

A goalie watches the whole play, not just the puck. When a skater moves into the shooting lane, even a small change may signal what's coming. A planet hunter is similar: the star looks almost unchanged, but tiny patterns are worth noticing. Not every clue means what you first think!

In Dad's engineering projects: test the model

Spacecraft engineers write predictions, measure what actually happens, and check whether the results agree. Your planet lab does the same: the circle-overlap equations predict a brightness dip. A program draws a graph. Your job is to see whether the graph makes sense and find situations where the simple model isn't enough.

Invent-a-mission challenge: imagine a tiny spacecraft that checks stars for dimming. What could make its measurements wrong — a wobbly camera, sunlight, or a changing detector? Write down how you'd test each one. That spacecraft is your imagined design, not a NASA mission.

Tech and reuse notes

What runs: one HTML file with its own CSS, SVG illustrations, and plain JavaScript. No accounts, trackers, fonts, CDN, frameworks, or paid libraries. Save the file and open it in a modern browser; the interactive lab works without Internet access. Source links need Internet only when you follow them.
