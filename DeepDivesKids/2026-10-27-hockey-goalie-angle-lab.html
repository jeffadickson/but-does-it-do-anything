01

The mystery on the ice

Imagine skater #28 racing toward the net. Goalie #66 is ready. The red posts are exactly where they were a moment ago—but each player sees a different puzzle.

🏒

For the skater

Moving toward the middle of the rink usually makes more of the net available to aim at. Moving closer changes the view again.

🥅

For the goalie

Staying on the puck's angle helps cover the net. Moving forward can look bigger to the shooter, but comes with risks.

Neither player needs to solve equations during a game. Their brains and practice help them make decisions—but a little geometry explains why those decisions work.

02

An angle is a slice of view

Imagine two invisible laser lines. One runs from the puck to the left post; the other goes from the puck to the right post. The space between them is the visible goal angle.

↔️

Move sideways

From far toward the boards, the two posts appear closer together. Your slice of view gets narrower.

🔍

Move closer

From the middle, the two posts spread farther apart in your view. Your slice gets wider.

🧱

Block a line

A goalie between the puck and the goal can hide part of the opening. The better the alignment, the more of the goal they can cover.

Important distinction: The net is still six feet wide! We are talking about how wide it appears from the puck, not about stretching or shrinking the actual goal.
03

Real hockey, real evidence

Documented, not imagined

Professional hockey analysts really study shot location and angle. A 2021 Seattle Kraken article explains that shots from nearer the middle generally offer shooters more of the net to target. It also explains why sealing the post helps against sharp-angle shots. Read the Kraken analysis ↗

Goalie coaches really practice staying square to the puck. A USA Hockey training article describes a shuffle drill that helps a goalie maintain a square position while moving sideways. See USA Hockey's coaching tips ↗

Positioning is not a single magic trick. An NHL report on goalie Linus Ullmark describes a small backward drift used to preserve mobility. Being too far out can make reacting to a sideways pass harder. Read the NHL technique report ↗

06

A real-world experiment

You can demonstrate hockey geometry in a hallway with paper, tape, and a toy figure. No real puck or stick needed.

Materials

Two paper cups for goalposts, a coin for the puck, a small toy goalie, tape for markers, a ruler, and a sheet to record guesses.

Make your mini rink

Place the cups six ruler inches apart. Set the coin 24 inches away, centered. Look from directly above the coin toward the goalposts.

  1. Move the coin to one side, keeping its distance from the goal roughly the same. Predict how the opening changes.
  2. Move the coin closer to the middle of the goal and observe the angle between the two cups.
  3. Put the toy goalie between the coin and cups. Shift it to one side. Can you see a larger gap on one side?

Scientist's notebook: Draw your two sight lines for each position. Say which result surprised you. Keep the experiment slow and safe—no hard shots indoors.

07

How the computer finds the angle

A computer can work out the angle between two lines using vectors. That sounds fancy, but a vector is just an arrow with a direction and length.

We place the middle of the goal at (0, 0), the left post at (−3, 0), and the right post at (3, 0). The shooter is at (x, y), measured in feet.

JavaScript · runs in your browser's consoleNo installation needed
// Two posts are six feet apart: -3 and +3.
// x = sideways position, y = distance from goal.
function goalAngleDegrees(x, y) {
  const left  = { x: -3 - x, y: -y };
  const right = { x:  3 - x, y: -y };

  // Compare how much the two arrows turn apart.
  const cross = left.x * right.y - left.y * right.x;
  const dot   = left.x * right.x + left.y * right.y;
  return Math.atan2(Math.abs(cross), dot) * 180 / Math.PI;
}

console.log(goalAngleDegrees(0, 24).toFixed(1));   // 14.3
console.log(goalAngleDegrees(0, 12).toFixed(1));   // 28.1
console.log(goalAngleDegrees(18, 24).toFixed(1));  // smaller
Think of it as2 arrows → 1 angle

Try changing 24 to 12. The shooter gets closer, and the angle grows. Change x from 0 to 18 while keeping y unchanged. The angle shrinks.

How the goalie barrier is modeled: Draw imaginary straight lines from the puck through the left and right edges of a flat 2.2-foot-wide barrier in front of the net. Where those lines hit the goal line gives a blocked segment. This is our toy model, not an official rule about goalie size.

09

What the simulation can't do

Every model has limits

Our little model only watches the puck and the goalie from overhead. It imagines an unmoving, flat barrier for the goalie. Real saves also depend on shot height, skating speed, pads and gloves, reaction time, deception, deflections, rebounds, passes, and the goalie's ability to move.

Not a save probability

Our "open feet" number is horizontal geometry only. It does not mean a shot has that chance of scoring.

Not a coaching prescription

Moving forward increases apparent coverage in this model, but a real goalie must stay balanced and ready for a pass.

Not a rink rulebook

The picture is a diagram with simplified markings. A real rink and goal crease have many more exact measurements.
