The mystery

👻 A six-inch monster? Or a six-foot monster?

Imagine cutting a small bat from black paper. You hold it in front of a flashlight, and suddenly an enormous flying creature stretches across your wall. Did your bat grow? Nope! You changed how the light spreads around it.

🕵️ Detective question

To make a shadow bigger, should you move the paper bat toward the flashlight or toward the wall?

Reveal the clue

Move it toward the flashlight. The bat blocks a bigger part of the widening light beam. We can test this instead of guessing.

🎨 Artist question

Could a shadow tell a story? Yes. Real artists have used carved shadow puppets with lamps, screens, narration, and music for centuries. See the Smithsonian's example.

How it works

💡 Light draws invisible straight lines

Imagine light traveling outward from one tiny point on a flashlight. When the paper bat blocks some of those lines, the wall behind the bat is darker: a shadow. The light lines that just skim the bat's top and bottom form two spreading rays.

The trick is a pair of similar triangles. A triangle from the light to the bat and a larger one from the light to the wall have matching angles. That gives us a prediction:

Shadow height = Bat height × (flashlight-to-wall distance ÷ flashlight-to-bat distance)
Example: an 8 cm bat, a wall 120 cm from the flashlight, and a bat 60 cm from the flashlight make a 16 cm shadow. Because 8 × (120 ÷ 60) = 16.

Move the bat halfway as far from the light, to 30 cm, and the model predicts a 32 cm shadow. The bat is still just 8 cm tall!

Important: This simple rule assumes a tiny point light, a flat upright cutout, and a wall parallel to it. Real flashlights have wider light sources, so shadow edges can look fuzzy. We explore that later.

Real-world maker lab

✂️ Build a Halloween shadow theater

What you need

A small flashlight, paper or cardstock, a pencil, tape, a popsicle stick, a ruler, and a light-colored wall. Ask a grown-up for help with scissors.

Your experiment

  1. Cut a bat, ghost, or hockey stick silhouette from paper. Measure its height.
  2. Point your flashlight at a clear wall in a dim room. Put the flashlight about 120 cm from the wall.
  3. Hold the cutout 60 cm from the flashlight and measure its shadow on the wall.
  4. Move the cutout to 30 cm from the flashlight. Measure again. Is the shadow nearly twice as tall?
  5. Change only one thing at a time. Write down your evidence.

Evidence, not guesses

🌍 Real shadows used by real people

🎭
Shadow theater is real art.

The Smithsonian has a Chinese lion shadow puppet made around 1850–1925. Its museum record describes figures moved in front of a lamp to project images onto a screen. Museum source.

🌑
Eclipse shadows are real.

NASA explains that the Moon can block sunlight and cast shadows on Earth. The darkest central region is the umbra; the partially shaded area is the penumbra. NASA source.

🦇
A cosmic “bat shadow” is real!

Hubble observed a giant bat-like shadow on a cloud, cast by a dusty disk around a young star called HBC 672. Its width spans roughly 200 solar-system lengths, according to NASA. NASA source.

🧠 One more real scientific use

NASA uses computer-generated light and shadow in shaded-relief maps of terrain. These images help viewers see slopes and landforms. That is a real visualization technique, not the Halloween simulator specifically. NASA Earth Observatory example.

Imagined, not verified deployments: A future haunted-house robot could plan where to cast a spooky shadow, or a rover simulator might use ray geometry to test whether an obstacle blocks a camera. Those are project ideas; we have not found evidence that RISCue, VeloSim, or any particular NASA mission uses this page's code.

A peek under the hood

💻 The computer's shadow trick

This whole page runs with ordinary HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The picture uses SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics). No outside coding library, login, installation, internet connection, or data collection is required after opening the file.

At the heart of the working simulator is this calculation:

function shadowHeight(batCm, wallCm, batDistanceCm) {
  if (batCm <= 0 || batDistanceCm <= 0 ||
      wallCm <= batDistanceCm) {
    throw new RangeError("Check the distances.");
  }
  return batCm * wallCm / batDistanceCm;
}

// Try it:
// shadowHeight(8, 120, 60) is 16.
// shadowHeight(8, 120, 30) is 32.

In words: height × wall distance ÷ bat distance. The simulator calls that same function when you change a slider. It then redraws the rays and shadow using the answer.

🔍 A mini debugging challenge

If somebody writes batCm * batDistanceCm / wallCm, will the shadow grow when the bat approaches the light? Try two values. If your computer's answer disagrees with an experiment, investigate the formula first.

Good scientists admit limitations

⚖️ What the model gets wrong

✅ What our model does well

For a tiny light source, a flat centered cutout parallel to a flat wall, and straight-line rays, the relative shadow height follows the similar-triangle equation. It makes repeatable predictions you can calculate yourself.

⚠️ What real life adds

Real flashlight bulbs are not perfect points. Their size can make blurred shadow edges (the penumbra). Paper can tilt. Walls may not be parallel. The simulator ignores brightness, detailed bat-wing geometry, reflections, and changing light colors.

Scientists call this a model: a simplified explanation that is useful for a specific question. The model is not a photograph, not a NASA mission design, and not an exact calculation of every shadow.

Connect the dots

🏒 What does this have to do with hockey and engineering?

For a hockey player or goalie: imagine the straight line from a shooter toward the goal. A goalie tries to cover the dangerous shooting angles. A puck isn't a beam of light and may deflect or curve, but practicing a line-of-sight diagram is a neat geometry connection.

For your dad's engineering projects: he works on software that models how systems behave—like RISCue and VeloSim. Our shadow simulator is a tiny example of the same idea: choose inputs, run a repeatable rule, predict an output, and test it. These are educational analogies, not claims that his software already implements this experiment.

For artists: the same measurements can help you stage a Halloween puppet show. You can use science to make a small character look mighty—and then use storytelling to make the audience gasp.
