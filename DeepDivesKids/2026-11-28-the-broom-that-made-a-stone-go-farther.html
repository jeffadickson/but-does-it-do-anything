01 / The mystery

Wait… the broom never touches the stone?

In curling, a player sends a heavy granite stone sliding toward a painted target called the house. Teammates hurry ahead with brushes. Their job is not to push the stone but to change how it slides. Imagine Quinn sending the stone and Reagan calling out when to sweep. Then swap jobs!

Today's big question: Can the same starting push produce two different stopping distances? Make your prediction before reading further.

Word power: friction is a force that resists sliding. A coefficient is a number that helps us describe how strong an effect is. A model is a simplified version of something real.
02 / Physics

Meet the invisible brake

1. Give it a start

A push gives a stone speed. Once the push ends, it keeps moving because of inertia.

2. Ice pushes back

Friction acts against sliding. It takes away moving energy, much of which becomes heat.

3. Sweep the path

Brushing can warm the ice surface and change its contact with the stone, often reducing friction. Less braking means a longer glide.

Ice is surprisingly complicated. Temperature, pressure, speed and the materials touching it all matter. Scientists investigate several mechanisms; there is no one simple explanation that works for every icy surface.

Math bonus: on level ground with a constant friction coefficient μ (say “mew”), braking acceleration is μ × 9.81 m/s² on Earth. Stopping distance is starting speed squared divided by twice that acceleration. Double the speed and the model predicts four times the stopping distance!

03 / Evidence notebook

Real teams, real measurements

Competition curling

World Curling explains that effective brushing can extend a stone's glide by two or three meters and help straighten its path. That is an example, not a guarantee for every throw. [1]

Brushes with sensors

In 2006, Marmo and colleagues recorded brush forces and speeds from Olympic-level curlers, then used laboratory experiments to study the heat from sweeping. They compared sweeping methods with a numerical model. [2]

Stopwatch science

In 2024, Sonobe and colleagues proposed a way to estimate friction from timed curling-stone motion and investigated how sweeping on one side affects the curve. The authors noted that curve control is still not fully understood. [3]

Skate-blade detective work

A 2021 ice-skate study discusses rink experiments and the limitations of existing explanations of slippery ice. Skate blades and granite stones touch ice differently, so don't simply copy a stone's friction value to a skate. [4]

Evidence versus imagination: the examples above are documented. Our pretend rink below uses invented coefficients and a fixed 30% friction reduction, not measured curling data. A future brush that automatically recommends when to sweep is an invention idea, not a claim that these studies built one.

05 / Hands-on, no ice required

Build a mini curling rink

Gather: a large plastic bottle cap, a sturdy smooth board or tray, a small book, a sheet of paper, a piece of cloth, tape, and a ruler. Ask an adult to help set up a clear tabletop or floor area with a towel at the far end to catch the cap. No real stones, slippery floors, or sharp objects needed.

  1. Rest one end of the board on the book to make a shallow ramp. Tape the ramp in place. Mark a release point near its top.
  2. Place the cap at the mark with its flat top touching the board, let go without pushing, and measure the distance it travels along a flat smooth landing surface from the ramp's end.
  3. Repeat three times. Then cover the flat landing surface with paper and finally with cloth, securing the edges away from the path so they do not catch the cap. Keep ramp angle and release point unchanged.
  4. One partner releases the cap; the other measures. Swap roles. Calculate each three-trial average by adding the distances and dividing by three.

06 / Code corner

Teach a computer how to stop

The following JavaScript is the calculation used in our lab, with input checking. Try it in a browser developer console with a grown-up. Speed is measured in meters per second and μ is dimensionless.

function glide(v, mu) {
  if (!Number.isFinite(v) || v < 0 ||
      !Number.isFinite(mu) || mu <= 0)
    throw new RangeError("Use a nonnegative speed and positive friction.");
  const a = 9.81 * mu;
  return { distance: v*v/(2*a), time: v/a };
}
console.log(glide(2.5, 0.04));
console.log(glide(2.5, 0.04 * 0.7));

First result: about 7.96 m and 6.37 s. Second result: about 11.38 m and 9.10 s. The difference is calculated before rounding. Zero friction is not allowed: the model would have no braking at all.

07 / Connections

From your rink to Dad's engineering desk

Quinn · the skater

Think about how a puck slides on freshly cleaned ice compared with ice covered in skate snow. A pass needs enough speed to reach your teammate without sailing past. Observe this during regular practice; don't try curling brushes on the hockey rink.

Reagan · the goalie

Goalie pads need to glide sideways, but skate edges need grip to stop and push off. Too little friction can be just as troublesome as too much. Notice the difference with your coach.

Dad · VeloSim and SharpHDL

Dad's digital engineering tools help people describe circuits and simulate what they do. Our little lab is a different kind of simulator, but it shares an important idea: use a model, test it against known answers, and clearly state where it stops being accurate.

Dad · spacecraft

On a spacecraft, moving parts such as deployment hinges also have friction. Engineers want to know that a mechanism moves when commanded and stops safely. This is an analogy, not a claim that our curling model predicts spacecraft behavior.

Honest tradeoffs

The lab assumes a level straight path, constant friction and no air resistance, spin, collisions or changing ice temperature. Real curling stones turn and can follow curved paths. Sweeping requires skill and effort; a longer glide is bad when the stone already has too much speed. A model is useful because it simplifies the world, but a good engineer checks its limits with real experiments.

08 / Championship round

Five questions for two detectives

Take turns answering aloud before opening each answer. Award one point for a correct answer and one bonus point for explaining why.

1. If the brush never pushes the stone, how can it go farther?

Brushing changes the ice contact and can reduce friction, so the stone slows less quickly.

2. At the same speed, which model lane stops first?

Ordinary ice, since it has the larger friction coefficient.

3. What happens to distance when starting speed doubles in this model?

It becomes four times as large because speed is squared.

4. Is our 30% sweeping reduction a measured result?

No. It is an invented teaching assumption, not an experimentally verified constant.

5. Why do we repeat the cap experiment?

To see how much measurements vary and reduce the influence of any single unusual trial.
