The great missing-puck mystery

Imagine Quinn and Reagan are helping with a hockey tournament. During practice, a special puck disappears into a giant snow-covered training field. Nobody saw where it landed. Three friendly lookout stations can each tell you how far away the puck is—but not which direction to look.

📡

Lookout A

“I know my position. I can measure how far away the treasure is!”

📡

Lookout B

“I have another distance clue. Together, our circles may cross in two places.”

📡

Lookout C

“My third clue helps you choose the right crossing.”

Today's magic word: trilateration (try-la-ter-AY-shun). It's finding a position using distances from known locations. That's different from triangulation, which uses angles. The NOAA teaching activity published by GPS.gov uses strings and circles to teach this very idea. See the official classroom activity ↗
2

One clue makes a circle

Suppose a lookout tells you: “The puck is exactly five squares away.” Does that mean five squares north? Nope! It could be five squares east, west, south, or anywhere in between. All those possible spots make a circle.

①

One distance

Every location along one circle is possible. Too many hiding places!

②

Two distances

Two circles usually meet in two points (sometimes one or none).

③

Three distances

A third circle can pick one shared point. Mystery solved—when measurements agree!

GPS satellites do not send the words “turn left!” Instead, they broadcast radio signals carrying timing and satellite-position information. Receivers estimate distance from signal travel time. Distance = speed × time. Because a receiver's clock is less accurate than the satellites' clocks, real GPS positioning normally needs signals from at least four satellites to determine position and time. The FAA explains why ↗

Science honesty check: Three perfect distances can solve a flat-map puzzle. GPS works in three dimensions and must solve a clock problem, so our game is a teaching model, not a working GPS receiver.

4

✏️ Offline mission: three pieces of string

No screen needed. A special trick makes this challenge surprisingly neat: all three distances are five grid squares!

Gather: graph paper, a pencil, a ruler, and a compass or a piece of string five grid squares long. Ask an adult to help if you use a sharp drawing compass.
  1. Draw a tiny map where x goes right and y goes up. Label 0 through 10 on both axes.
  2. Mark Lookout A at (0, 0), B at (6, 0), and C at (0, 8).
  3. Draw a circle of radius 5 squares around A, then one around B, then one around C.
  4. Find the spot shared by all three circles. Write its coordinate, then open the answer below to check your work.
🔐 Open the paper-mission answer

(3, 4)! It is 3 squares right and 4 squares up from A, making a 3–4–5 right triangle. From B, it is 3 left and 4 up; from C, 3 right and 4 down. All three distances are 5. If you got it, you just used trilateration.

Try an experiment: use only A and B first. How many crossing points do you find? Now add C. Why does the third circle help? Can a friend invent a new mystery with three different lookout positions?
5

Real technology vs. imaginary missions

✅

Documented, real uses

Flying airplanes: the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration describes satellite-navigation signals and the four-satellite positioning process used by GPS receivers. That's real navigation engineering. FAA ↗

Watching earthquakes: the U.S. Geological Survey uses carefully measured GPS positions to study how ground near faults moves over time. Tiny changes matter! USGS ↗

Teaching with strings: NOAA made a classroom trilateration activity now provided by GPS.gov, using flat maps and string circles—much like our paper adventure. GPS.gov ↗

💭

Fun ideas, not adoption claims

Hockey: Quinn could imagine tracking a practice puck while Reagan guards the goal. Real hockey pucks do not magically transmit satellite ranging signals! A real tracking system would need sensors and appropriate equipment.

Dad's engineering: spacecraft software and simulation tools such as VeloSim can be tested with invented measurement clues. RISCue could organize the incoming “clue arrived” events. These are ideas for projects, not claims that either project uses this particular game or GPS.

Find the hidden snack: invent three distances between your backyard landmarks and play treasure detective with a friend. No actual satellites required!

Why isn't GPS always perfect? Buildings and trees can block signals; reflections can fool a receiver into thinking the signal took longer to arrive. The atmosphere and the positions of satellites also matter. That's why our optional “wobble” mode is useful: it makes a difficult idea visible, though its errors are invented, not a real GPS error model. GPS.gov explains accuracy ↗

👩‍💻 Peek inside the code

The interactive map uses plain JavaScript. Here is its distance idea. Math.hypot is a built-in JavaScript function that finds the length of a diagonal using the Pythagorean theorem.

// A lookout is at (3, 3).
// Our hidden treasure is at (6, 7).
const lookout = { x: 3, y: 3 };
const treasure = { x: 6, y: 7 };

const sideways = treasure.x - lookout.x; // 3
const upwards  = treasure.y - lookout.y; // 4
const distance = Math.hypot(sideways, upwards);

console.log(distance); // 5

// Every point 5 units from the lookout
// lies on a circle of radius 5.

⚙️ What's in it?

One HTML file, built-in SVG shapes, CSS styling, and vanilla JavaScript. No downloaded packages, external fonts, account, or network connection needed for the game.

🧪 What isn't in it?

No radio receiver, GPS chip, real map service, or actual satellites. All distances and treasure locations are fictional teaching data.

🔧 Can I change it?

Yes. Save the HTML file, open it with a text editor, and change the five treasure coordinates in the script. Test whether your new points fall on the 0–20 grid!

Tradeoffs: perfect distances and a flat 21×21 map make the problem approachable but leave out GPS clock errors, Earth's curvature, obstacles, radio noise, and real measurement uncertainty. The app rounds distance labels to one decimal place but draws exact-radius circles in normal mode. It cannot give a real-world location.
