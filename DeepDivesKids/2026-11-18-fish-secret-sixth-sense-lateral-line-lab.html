Mission 1 • Mystery

Can a fish feel something it can't see?

Imagine snorkeling in a lake. A little animal swims nearby, stirring the water. You might not notice anything. But many fish have a special sensory system that detects water moving close to their bodies—even when the water is dark or murky.

👀 Eyes

They detect light. Seeing can be tricky in darkness or cloudy water.

〰️ Lateral line

It senses changes in local water motion. It isn't reading minds, and it isn't seeing the future. [1][3]

Vocabulary clue: Lateral means “at the side.” Fish often have sensory structures along their sides and heads. This is not the same thing as their ears.

Mission 2 • How it works

A line of microscopic water detectives

1

Water moves

A passing animal, a current, or a swirling eddy changes the flow near a fish.

2

Hairs bend

Tiny sensory groups called neuromasts contain hair cells that respond to movement in the water.

3

Signals travel

Nerves carry information that the fish can combine with other senses to guide its behavior.

Some detectors sit on the body's surface. Others are inside fluid-filled canals that connect to outside water through small pores. Think of many measuring stations, not one superhero button. [1][3]

A fish's lateral line feels nearby flow. The big circles in our cartoon lab are just a fun way to show that a clue travels through water; real underwater flow does not always form perfect circles.

Mission 3 • Scientists measured it

Real fish, real experiments, real numbers

13–15 ms

Quick as a blink? Much quicker!

In a 2009 experiment, the most common response delay for young zebrafish exposed to water-flow pulses was about 13–15 milliseconds—thousandths of a second. Scientists showed that lateral-line hair cells helped. [1]

70% / 5%

One sense made a big difference

In a 2013 predation study, young zebrafish with working lateral lines escaped about 70% of predator strikes on average. When the lateral line was experimentally disabled, the mean dropped to about 5%. These results describe those study groups—not all fish everywhere. [2]

Scientists compared groups with working and disabled sensors, then counted the results. Good science asks not just “Did it happen?” but “What changed, and did we repeat the test?”

Mission 5 • Hands-on science

Make your own ripple detective

Gather: a shallow tray, water, a little cork or small floating piece of paper, a spoon, a ruler, a towel, and a grown-up for setup. Never put electricity near your tray. Do not use live fish.

  1. Put a little water in the tray and float the cork in the middle.
  2. Gently dip your spoon into the water a short distance from the cork. Watch the cork carefully.
  3. Move the spoon farther away. Keep your dip as similar as possible. Try each distance five times; write down how often you notice a movement.
  4. Try a small spoon movement and a slightly bigger movement. Change just one thing at a time.
  5. Place a cardboard screen between the observer and the spoon (above, not in, the water). Can the observer guess when a ripple happened just by watching the cork?

Important difference: your cork responds to surface waves, but fish sense more complicated flow near their bodies. The experiment teaches that moving water carries clues; it does not prove a cork is a real lateral-line organ.

Mission 6 • Fish-inspired engineering

Underwater robots can borrow ideas from nature

✔ Documented real engineering

MIT researchers attached arrays of tiny pressure sensors to experimental vessels and tested their response to currents and swirling flow in a towing tank. This was actual testing, not an imagined gadget. [4]

✧ Ideas still to test

A future robot might use fish-like sensing to avoid rocks, detect a passing animal, or make smarter decisions about currents. Our game does not demonstrate any of those capabilities in a real lake.

MIT Sea Grant also described a research project to explore whether arrays of fish-inspired sensors could help underwater vehicles use less battery energy. That's a research goal, not a guaranteed saving. [5]

🏒 For Quinn and Reagan

A goalie watches a puck and a skater reads teammates and opponents. They detect clues and react. A trout has a different set of clues, including motion in the water. It can't predict the future; it senses the present.

🛰️ At Dad's engineering desk

GRACE-C plans to measure tiny differences between two spacecraft to learn how Earth's gravity and water change. Projects like VeloSim also use models that must be checked. Today's simple pattern is sensor → measurement → decision—not a real satellite simulator. [6]

Mission 7 • Your code

Three little equations, one big question

Today's lab needs no external libraries or downloads. It uses HTML, CSS, SVG, and JavaScript already supported by modern browsers. Here's the exact rule that makes our toy decision:

function toyDetector(power, distance, sensorsOn) {
  const signal = 1.4 * power / (distance * distance + 1);
  const alert = sensorsOn && signal >= 0.38;
  return { signal, alert };
}

// For power=3, distance=3, sensorsOn=true:
// signal is approximately 0.42, alert is true

The score gets smaller when the distance grows. That makes a fun game, but our squared-distance formula is not a proven law for fish. Real underwater currents have different shapes. Change the sliders and see whether the decision changes.

Mission 9 • Model versus reality

The smartest engineers know what's missing

✅ What we can learn

We can compare near and far, strong and weak, and sensors on and off. We can ask one clear question and repeat the test.

⚠️ What we cannot claim

Our pretend score does not measure real fluid flow. Real fish combine many senses, real currents have swirling three-dimensional motion, and actual responses vary.
