The Telescope That Folded Into a Rocket!

Can a giant space machine fit through a tiny doorway? Grab a sheet of paper and discover the clever geometry of unfolding.

01 · The tiny doorway problem

Imagine arriving at the rink with a net wider than the door. You could leave it outside, build it inside, or design it to fold. A rocket has a similar problem: its protective nose cone has a limited amount of room.

A space telescope needs a big mirror to collect faint light. A spacecraft may need large solar panels to make electricity. The trick is to pack these big parts into a smaller space, launch them, and open them safely afterward. Engineers call this deployment.

Mission question: Can you squeeze a long strip into a space just 8 centimeters wide without cutting it or stretching it? Can you open it again?

02 · Mountains and valleys

Mountain fold

A crease that rises like a ridge when you look at the chosen face of your paper.

Valley fold

A crease that dips like a canyon when you look at that same face.

Accordion fold

Alternate mountains and valleys. The sheet bends into a zigzag and becomes shorter from left to right.

Origami is the art of folding paper. Space engineers can borrow its patterns, but metal panels need hinges, room for wires and sometimes motors and latches. Paper folds and spacecraft joints are not the same thing.

A tilted panel still has the same length. Only its horizontal shadow becomes shorter. A row of tilted panels therefore occupies less width, but more height.

03 · Fold Lab: fit through the fairing!

This is an imaginary spacecraft with identical 4 cm panels. Each panel tilts by the same angle above or below horizontal. The model ignores thickness, hinges and hardware. The dashed rectangle is an imaginary 8 cm wide, 5 cm tall packing envelope, not a real rocket specification.

Does not fit: 12.0 cm wide. The packing envelope is 8 cm wide and 5 cm tall.

Two-person challenge

One person chooses a panel count; the other finds the smallest whole-number tilt that fits. Switch jobs. Can you predict why ten panels need a steeper angle than four?

How the computer measures the width

At 0°, each panel contributes all 4 cm to width. At 60°, each contributes just 2 cm. The cosine function gives the horizontal fraction; sine gives the height fraction. The browser uses radians internally, so we convert degrees first.

angleInRadians = degrees * Math.PI / 180;
width = panelCount * 4 * Math.cos(angleInRadians);
height = 4 * Math.sin(angleInRadians);

The same equations power the lab. We compare the unrounded values against the envelope.

04 · Build a paper spacecraft

Materials: a rectangular strip of paper at least 24 cm long, a ruler and pencil. Ask an adult to cut the strip if needed. No sharp tools or electricity required after that.

  1. Mark six 4 cm panels along a 24 cm strip (or use 2 cm panels if your strip is shorter, and halve the target widths).
  2. Make a crease at every mark between panels, alternating mountain and valley folds. Open the strip flat and measure its length.
  3. Set the zigzag on a table with its side facing you. Squeeze gently until it spans about 12 cm. Estimate its height. Try again at 8 cm.
  4. Measure the narrowest width you can achieve without crushing it, then unfold it again. Repeat three times.
  5. Compare with the lab. If your folded strip is shorter or wider than expected, look for unequal angles, paper thickness or bent panels.

Bonus design challenge: fold a second strip with fewer panels. Which one packs more easily? Which one is easier to unfold without a jam? Change only one thing at a time.

05 · What engineers actually built

Flown spacecraft

James Webb Space Telescope

Webb's 6.5-meter primary mirror has 18 segments. Its mirror wings and sunshield were packed for launch, then deployed in space. NASA documents the completed deployment and subsequent commissioning. Its real hinges are not our paper accordion.

NASA: Webb and origami [1]
Ground prototype

JPL + BYU solar array

In 2014, researchers described a flower-like folded solar-array design. Their tabletop prototype at 1/20 scale opened to a diameter of 1.25 meters. This was a research model, not evidence that this particular array flew.

JPL: solar power, origami-style [2]
Development prototype

Starshade

JPL documented a folded 20-meter starshade prototype built to investigate a separate spacecraft that could hide a distant star's glare from a telescope. The prototype is real; an operational mission is not established by this source.

JPL: furled starshade prototype [3]

Inventor's corner (imagined): A foldable hockey practice target or an expanding robot shelter could be fun designs to sketch. These are ideas, not products or tested NASA hardware.

What can go wrong?

Extra hinges can introduce jams. Thick panels need clearance; cables must not get pinched. A packed spacecraft also needs to survive launch shaking. A large deployed surface may flex, so engineers test its strength, motion and alignment. A smaller packed width alone does not prove a better design.

06 · Hockey and Dad's engineering workshop

A skater and a goalie both fold their knees and elbows to change their body's shape. Their bones do not get shorter. That's the same basic idea as our tilted panel, although a person is much more complicated! Imagine designing a small, foldable practice goal that has to fit in a car.

At JPL, spacecraft engineers have to plan how hardware fits, moves and behaves. Dad's VeloSim project is about simulating digital circuits; our fold lab is a different kind of simulation, but both use rules to predict behavior before building hardware. His SharpHDL work describes digital hardware precisely. Imagine a simple controller with three states: packed, opening and open. It should never report open until a sensor confirms the move. This is a classroom design idea, not a claim that either project controls Webb.

Design review: Draw your controller as three boxes with arrows. What should it do if the sensor never says open? A safe design might stop, record a problem and wait for help rather than pretending everything worked.

07 · Mission control quiz

1. Did the panels shrink when they tilted?

No. Their horizontal span shrank, but each panel remains 4 cm long.

2. Why not just fold every spacecraft as tightly as possible?

Real hardware has thickness and moving parts. A tight package may jam, pinch wires or be too weak when opened.

3. Which of today's examples definitely flew: Webb or the 2014 JPL/BYU flower-like prototype?

Webb flew and deployed in space. The JPL/BYU example in the cited report was a tabletop prototype.

4. At 60°, how wide are eight 4 cm panels?

Each contributes 2 cm horizontally, so 8 × 2 = 16 cm.

5. Can you use an origami starshade to look at the Sun?

No. Never use paper or this model as eye protection for viewing the Sun.
