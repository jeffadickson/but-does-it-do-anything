🕵️ A mystery hidden in a log

Imagine you discover a fallen branch with a beautiful spiral of light and dark stripes. Each stripe seems like a page in a diary. But who wrote it?

The tree did! For many species in places with clear growing seasons, a new ring of wood forms each year. The inner rings are older; the outer rings are younger. The scientific word is dendrochronology (den-dro-kruh-NOL-uh-jee): putting dates on tree rings.

💭 Predict

Which ring is the newest: the one near the middle or the one near the bark?

Answer: The outside ring, just under the bark.

🎯 Your mission

Explore a pretend 12-year-old tree, spot two years of slow growth, then decide whether the evidence proves there was a drought.

🪵 How a tree makes its time machine

  1. Spring and early growing season: A tree grows new wood just beneath its bark. In many trees, early wood looks lighter.
  2. Later in the growing season: The wood may become denser and darker. Together, those parts often form one annual growth ring.
  3. Next growing season: Another ring forms outside the old one. The trunk gets wider as the tree grows.
  4. Scientists compare patterns: Narrow and wide rings in several trees can be matched, helping experts check dates. This is called crossdating.
🌱 Important: One ring per year is a useful rule for many temperate trees, not a law for every tree everywhere. Missing rings, extra-looking boundaries, and confusing patterns can occur. Scientists use careful comparisons.

🌍 Real scientists really do this

🌦️ NOAA: climate clues

NOAA explains that researchers measure ring widths, compare many samples, and check them against observed weather. Some trees are especially sensitive to water; others respond strongly to temperature. Read NOAA's explanation.

🔥 National parks: fire clues

In Sequoia and Kings Canyon, National Park Service researchers use accurately dated tree rings and fire scars to reconstruct when some past fires happened. See the park's research.

📚 NOAA: real research data

NOAA maintains the International Tree-Ring Data Bank, with measurements from thousands of sites. Those are real research records—not the invented numbers in our game. Explore the data archive.

Evidence vs. invention: The examples above are documented research activities. The two trees and all 2014–2025 measurements below are made-up teaching data. We are practicing the method, not reporting what happened to a real forest.

✏️ Screen-free experiment: Invent a forest diary

You need: paper, a pencil, two colored pencils, and a ruler. No tools, trees, or sharp equipment needed.

  1. Draw a tiny circle. This is the center of your pretend tree.
  2. Draw six more rings outside it. Make the gaps wide, narrow, wide, narrow, narrow, wide. Color alternating areas lightly and darkly.
  3. Label the oldest ring Year 1 and the newest ring Year 7. Start at the center.
  4. Draw another “tree” with a similar wide-narrow pattern. Can a friend line up the two histories?
  5. Tell a scientist's story: “The ring is narrow” is an observation. “Maybe it had less water” is a hypothesis, not a proven fact.
Forest care: Look at fallen wood from a safe distance; do not cut or core a living tree or take protected wood from a park. On a Yosemite walk, look for pinecones, bark textures, and giant trees without removing anything.

💻 The tiny computer detective

The interactive picture is made with ordinary browser JavaScript, SVG circles, and a fixed array. No downloads, accounts, or outside libraries are required.

This working JavaScript example finds the years when the invented Tree A grew 2 mm or less. You can paste it into a browser console:

const years = Array.from({ length: 12 }, (_, i) => 2014 + i);
const widths = [4, 6, 3, 7, 8, 4, 2, 2, 5, 9, 6, 4];
const tinyYears = years.filter((year, i) => widths[i] <= 2);
console.log(tinyYears.join(", ")); // 2020, 2021

Change one thing: What happens if the limit changes from 2 to 3? Predict before checking! The code does not know why growth was slow; it only compares numbers.

🥅 Hockey pattern hunters

Quinn and Reagan can notice repeated patterns in a hockey shift: when was a pass fast, when was it slow, and what changed? Like tree rings, a pattern is a clue, not proof of its cause.

🏞️ Yosemite explorers

Yosemite has giant sequoia groves. Before a nature walk, remember that a living tree is more valuable than a souvenir. National Park Service guide.

🛰️ Dad's GRACE-C engineering

NASA's planned GRACE-C satellites will measure tiny changes in Earth's gravity to help scientists track moving water and ice. Tree rings and satellites are different kinds of evidence; scientists compare observations instead of guessing. NASA mission page.

🤖 Dad's simulations

In projects like VeloSim and RISCue, engineers test a model repeatedly. Our fake tree widths are also a repeatable model: press the same year, get the same answer. That doesn't make the invented data real.

⚖️ What the tree time machine cannot do

  • It cannot directly read a rain gauge from the past. A narrow ring may be linked to water, cold, pests, competition, age, or damage.
  • It cannot date every tree perfectly just by counting. Species and environment matter; researchers crossdate and check unusual rings.
  • Our model cannot measure a real forest. The shapes are circles, widths are invented, and the “tiny year” cutoff is a game rule—not a scientific threshold.
  • One tree is rarely enough. Scientists gather many samples and compare them with other evidence.

Being a good scientist means saying both what you know and what you do not yet know.
